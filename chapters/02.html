Chapter 2: Rolling Downhill

The problem of the million knobs

Imagine you are standing in front of a machine with a million knobs on it. You feed in a picture of a handwritten digit, a seven, say, and the machine lights up one of ten bulbs to tell you which digit it thinks it saw. At the moment the knobs are set at random, and the machine says "three". Your job is to turn the knobs until the machine gets it right, not just for this seven but for sixty thousand different handwritten digits.

You could try the obvious thing. Pick a knob, turn it a hair, and see whether the machine gets slightly better or slightly worse. Then put it back, and try the next knob. After a million such experiments you would know which way to nudge every knob, and you could nudge them all a little, and then begin again. This would work. It is also hopeless. Each round costs you a million runs of the machine, and you will need many thousands of rounds. For a modern network with a hundred billion weights, a single round would take longer than the age of the universe.

What you want is some way to find out, in one go, how every knob affects the outcome. Not a million experiments, but one. That is what the algorithm called backpropagation gives you, and it is not an exaggeration to say that everything in modern artificial intelligence stands on it. Before we get to it, though, we need two preliminary ideas: a way to measure how wrong the machine is, and a strategy for using that measurement.

How wrong are we?

The first idea is the loss function. A loss function takes the network's output and the correct answer, and returns a single number that says how wrong the output was. Zero means perfect. Bigger means worse.

You already know one loss function: the sum of squared errors you use when fitting a line. If the network outputs a number and the right answer was a different number, take the difference and square it. For classification problems, like the digit recogniser, a different loss is used, which we will meet properly in chapter five, but the principle is identical. The network outputs a confidence for each possible answer, and the loss is small when it put high confidence on the right one and large when it did not.

Average the loss over all your training examples, and you get one number that summarises how well the network, with its current weights, fits the data. Change the weights, and that number changes. So the average loss is itself a function, a function of the weights. And this is the mental picture I want you to build. Imagine a landscape. The position on the map is the setting of the weights. The altitude at each position is the loss. High ground is bad; low ground is good. Training a network means finding your way to a low valley.

Of course, the map does not have two dimensions. It has as many dimensions as there are weights, a million, or a hundred billion. But the picture of a landscape is still the right one, and physicists have a head start here, because it is precisely the picture of a potential energy surface. The system we are about to describe behaves like a ball rolling through thick honey on that surface, always sliding toward lower energy.

Feeling the slope

The second idea is the strategy, and it is called gradient descent.

You are on this landscape, and you are blindfolded. You cannot see where the valleys are. But you can feel the slope of the ground under your feet. So you do the natural thing: work out which direction is most steeply downhill, take a small step in that direction, and repeat.

Mathematically, the slope is the gradient: the list of partial derivatives of the loss with respect to every weight. Each entry answers the question, "if I increased this one weight by a tiny amount, and left all the others alone, how much would the loss change?" Collect all of those answers, and you have a vector that points in the direction of steepest ascent. Step the opposite way.

How big a step? That is set by a number called the learning rate, and it is the first example we have met of what is called a hyperparameter: a setting that is not learned by the network, but chosen by the human, usually by trial and error. Too small a learning rate, and training takes forever. Too large, and you leap clean across the valley and up the other side, and the loss, instead of falling, bounces around or explodes. A great deal of the practical craft of this field, more than its practitioners might like to admit, consists of getting numbers like the learning rate right.

So the whole of training comes down to one question. How do we compute the gradient? How do we get those hundred billion partial derivatives without doing a hundred billion experiments?

The chain rule, run backwards

Here is the setup. A network is a long chain of simple operations. The input goes into the first layer of neurons, which multiply by weights, add, and squash. Their outputs go into the second layer, which does the same. And so on, until the last layer produces the output, and the loss function turns that into a single number. It is a function of a function of a function, nested many levels deep.

You know how to differentiate a function of a function. That is the chain rule. If the loss depends on the output of the last layer, and that output depends on the layer before, then the sensitivity of the loss to the earlier layer is the product of the two sensitivities along the way.

The insight of backpropagation is about the order in which you do the bookkeeping. First, run the network forwards on an example, in the ordinary way, and remember every intermediate value: the output of every neuron. That is called the forward pass. Now start at the far end. Ask: how sensitive is the loss to each output of the final layer? That is easy; it is just the derivative of the loss function. Next, step back one layer. For each neuron in the second-to-last layer, ask how sensitive the loss is to its output. Well, that neuron influences the loss only by way of the final-layer neurons it feeds. You already know the sensitivity of the loss to each of those. So you just combine them, weighted by how strongly this neuron influences each one, and that strength is given by, of all things, the weights connecting them. Step back another layer and repeat. This is the backward pass.

At each stage you are reusing the work done for the layer after. A message of blame flows backwards through the network, from the output toward the input, and as it passes each weight, it tells that weight exactly how much it contributed to the error and in which direction. One forward pass, one backward pass, and you have the complete gradient: every partial derivative, for every weight, at a cost of roughly twice that of simply running the network. Not a hundred billion runs. Two.

That is the solution to the credit assignment problem from the last chapter. How do you know what a hidden neuron, buried in the middle, should have done? You do not need to know what it should have done. You only need to know which direction of change would have reduced the error, and the chain rule tells you.

If you work in imaging or inverse problems, you may have recognised this. It is the adjoint method. When you compute the gradient of a data-mismatch term with respect to an image by applying the adjoint of your forward operator, as in iterative tomographic reconstruction, where back-projection is the adjoint of projection, you are doing the same thing. Backpropagation is the adjoint method applied to a deep stack of non-linear operations. Applied mathematicians call the general technique reverse-mode automatic differentiation.

There is one technical consequence that matters for the history. For any of this to work, the network must be differentiable. Rosenblatt's neurons used a hard step for their activation function, either zero or one. The derivative of a step is zero everywhere, except at the jump, where it is undefined. No slope means no signal to follow. So the step had to be replaced with something smooth, at first an S-shaped curve called the sigmoid, which rises gently from zero to one. That small change, from a cliff to a ramp, was the price of admission.

Who invented it?

Backpropagation has a tangled paternity, as fundamental ideas often do. The underlying mathematics was developed in control theory around 1960, for computing optimal rocket trajectories. A Finnish master's student named Seppo Linnainmaa described reverse-mode automatic differentiation in 1970. An American, Paul Werbos, proposed in his 1974 doctoral thesis that it could be used to train neural networks, and found that almost no one was interested.

The version that changed the field appeared in the journal Nature in October 1986, under the title "Learning representations by back-propagating errors". It had three authors: David Rumelhart, a psychologist at the University of California in San Diego; Ronald Williams; and a young Englishman named Geoffrey Hinton.

Hinton is as close as this story has to a central character, so let me introduce him. He came from a remarkable family; his great-great-grandfather was George Boole, the inventor of the Boolean logic on which all digital computers run. He had drifted through physiology, philosophy and psychology at Cambridge, worked for a time as a carpenter, and finally took a doctorate in artificial intelligence at Edinburgh in the nineteen-seventies, at exactly the moment when neural networks were least fashionable. His supervisor tried, repeatedly, to talk him out of them. Hinton had a conviction, which he held with what can only be called stubbornness for the next thirty-five years, that the brain must work by adjusting the strengths of connections between neurons, and that the way to build an intelligent machine was to do the same.

What the 1986 paper did was not so much to invent the algorithm as to demonstrate what it was good for. And the word to notice in the title is "representations". The authors showed that when you train a multi-layer network by backpropagation, the hidden neurons in the middle spontaneously come to represent meaningful features of the problem, features that nobody programmed in. In one example, a network given facts about two family trees, such as who is whose mother and who is whose uncle, developed internal units that encoded things like a person's generation, or which branch of the family they belonged to. The network had not been told that those concepts existed. It had invented them, because they were useful for getting the answers right.

That is the idea we will pick up in the next chapter. But first, some practicalities that will be with us for the rest of the book.

Stochastic gradient descent

The landscape I described is defined by averaging the loss over all your training data. For a modern language model, the training data is a fair fraction of all the text ever written. Computing the true gradient would mean running the forward and backward pass over all of it before taking a single step.

Nobody does that. Instead, you grab a small random handful of examples, called a mini-batch, perhaps a few dozen or a few thousand. You compute the gradient just for those, take a step, and then grab another handful. The gradient from a handful is a noisy estimate of the true gradient, in the same way that a poll of a thousand people is a noisy estimate of an election. But it points roughly the right way, and you can take a thousand rough steps in the time it would take to compute one perfect one. This is called stochastic gradient descent, universally abbreviated to SGD. One complete pass through the training data is called an epoch.

The noise turns out to be not just tolerable but helpful. A physicist would say it acts like a temperature. It jostles the system, shaking it out of narrow crevices and letting it settle in broad, flat valleys. And there is good evidence that broad, flat valleys are precisely where you want to be: solutions there tend to work better on new data than solutions found in sharp, narrow pits.

A few refinements are worth knowing by name. One is momentum: rather than following the current slope alone, let the weights accumulate velocity like a heavy ball, so that the motion smooths over small bumps and speeds up along consistent downhill directions. Another, from 2014, is an algorithm called Adam, which gives every individual weight its own automatically adjusted step size. Adam, or a close variant of it, is what trains almost every large model today. When you hear researchers mention the optimiser, this is the thing they mean: the specific recipe for turning gradients into weight updates.

Why does this work at all?

There is something that ought to bother you about all this. Gradient descent only ever goes downhill. So it should get stuck in the first hollow it comes to, the nearest local minimum, which might be far higher than the deepest valley. For a long time this was regarded as a decisive objection. The landscapes of neural networks are not simple bowls. They are fantastically rugged. Mathematicians knew how to guarantee good results for bowl-shaped problems, which they call convex, and neural networks were emphatically non-convex. For much of the nineteen-nineties and two-thousands, this was a respectable reason to dismiss the whole approach.

It turned out not to matter, and the reason is a nice piece of high-dimensional intuition. To be stuck in a true local minimum, the ground must curve upward in every direction you could possibly move. In two dimensions that is common; any dimple will do. But in a billion dimensions, it requires that all billion independent directions curve upward at once. If each direction is even roughly a coin toss, that essentially never happens. What you find instead are saddle points, places that curve up in some directions and down in others, and from a saddle, there is always a way out. The few true minima that do exist tend to be about as good as one another. In very high dimensions, the landscape is less like a mountain range full of traps and more like a vast, gently connected system of valleys.

Nobody proved this in advance. People tried it, and it worked, and the theoretical understanding came afterwards and is still incomplete. That is a pattern you will see repeatedly in this field. It is an empirical science, closer in spirit to experimental physics than to mathematics, and the theory is usually running to catch up with the experiments.

The elephant in the room

There is a second thing that ought to bother you, and it will bother you more if you are a physicist.

John von Neumann is supposed to have said that with four parameters he could fit an elephant, and with five he could make it wiggle its trunk. The point is one that every experimentalist has had drilled into them: a model with too many free parameters will fit anything, including the noise in your data, and will therefore predict nothing. This is called overfitting. A network with a billion parameters, trained on a million examples, has every opportunity to simply memorise the examples. Its loss on the training data would go to zero, and it would be useless on anything new.

The basic defence is methodological, and it is so central to the culture of machine learning that it is worth stating plainly. You never judge a model by its performance on the data it was trained on. You hold some data back, called the test set, which the model never sees during training, and you measure performance on that. What you care about is generalisation: how well the model does on examples it has not seen. Nearly every claim you will ever hear about a model's ability is, underneath, a claim about performance on some held-out test set, known as a benchmark, and a surprising number of arguments in the field come down to whether a particular benchmark was really held out.

But the deeper puzzle remains. By all the classical statistical wisdom, these gigantic models should overfit catastrophically. They do not. They generalise remarkably well, and they generalise better as they get bigger. Why that should be is one of the genuinely deep questions about this technology, and I will come back to it in chapter four, once we have seen the networks that forced the question.

The loop

Let me put the pieces together, because this loop is the heartbeat of everything that follows.

You start with a network whose weights are random numbers. You take a batch of examples. You run them through the network: the forward pass. You compare the outputs with the right answers and compute the loss. You run backpropagation, the backward pass, to get the gradient: the direction in which each weight should move to reduce the loss. You move every weight a small step in that direction. Then you take another batch, and do it again. And again. Millions of times.

That is training. It is not a metaphor for something more sophisticated. The most capable artificial intelligence systems in the world were made by this loop, running for months on tens of thousands of specialised chips. Nobody tells the network what features to look for or what concepts to form. There is only a number that measures error, and a procedure that relentlessly makes that number smaller. Everything the network knows, it knows because knowing it helped push the loss downhill.

Which raises the question of what, exactly, the hidden layers in the middle come to know. Why do we need them, why should there be many, and what are they doing? That is where we go next.

The maths in brief

  • Loss over a dataset: L(w) = (1/N) Σ ℓ(f(xᵢ; w), yᵢ).
  • Gradient descent update: w ← w − η ∇L(w), with learning rate η.
  • Backprop for one layer a = f(z), z = W x + b: given ∂L/∂a, compute δ = ∂L/∂a ⊙ f′(z), then ∂L/∂W = δ xᵀ, ∂L/∂b = δ, and pass back ∂L/∂x = Wᵀ δ. Note the transpose: the backward pass uses the adjoint of the forward linear map.
  • Momentum: v ← βv − η∇L, w ← w + v. Adam additionally divides each weight's step by a running estimate of that weight's gradient magnitude.
