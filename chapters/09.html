Chapter 9: The Bitter Lesson and the Scaling Laws

An essay of a thousand words

In March of 2019, a computer scientist named Richard Sutton posted a short essay on his personal website. Sutton is one of the founders of the field of reinforcement learning, which we will come to in chapter eleven, and he has a reputation for thinking in decades. The essay was barely more than a thousand words long. It was called "The Bitter Lesson", and it may be the most influential piece of informal writing in the history of the field. If you listen to researchers talking among themselves, you will hear it referred to all the time, often as a single word: something is, or is not, bitter-lesson-pilled.

Sutton's argument was a reading of seventy years of history. Over and over, he said, researchers in artificial intelligence have tried to make their systems better by building in their own knowledge of the problem. Chess programmers encoded what grandmasters know about chess. Speech researchers encoded what linguists know about phonemes and the vocal tract. Vision researchers designed features to detect edges and shapes, based on what they understood about seeing. This always helps, at first. It is also satisfying to the researcher, since it makes use of expertise.

And then, over and over, those systems were overtaken and made obsolete by methods that had none of that knowledge in them: general methods that simply made use of more computation. Chess fell in 1997, not to a program that understood chess as a grandmaster does, but to massive search. Speech recognition was conquered by statistical methods trained on large amounts of data, and one of the pioneers of that work is said to have remarked that every time he fired a linguist, the performance of his system improved. We saw the same thing in chapter four, when the hand-built features of computer vision were swept away by AlexNet, and in chapter six, when the vast machinery of statistical machine translation was replaced by a neural network within nine months.

The reason, Sutton argued, is Moore's law, or more exactly the long exponential fall in the cost of computation. Methods that rely on human knowledge are limited by human effort, which does not grow. Methods that rely on computation ride an exponential. Over any sufficiently long period, the exponential wins. Only two kinds of method, he said, seem to scale without limit as computation increases: search, and learning.

Why "bitter"? Because the lesson is a hard one for researchers to accept. It says that the clever, insightful work of encoding your own understanding into a system is, in the long run, wasted, and worse than wasted, since it gets in the way. What succeeds is general-purpose methods and brute scale, and their success feels like no credit to anyone's intelligence. Recall the idea of inductive bias from chapter three: the assumptions that are built into an architecture. Sutton's essay is, in effect, an argument that one should build in as little as possible. And the Transformer, with its nearly complete lack of assumptions about its input, is the bitter lesson made into an architecture.

Not everyone accepts this. But a few organisations have bet everything on it, and to see why they felt able to do so, we need to look at some graphs.

Straight lines on log paper

In January 2020, a few months before GPT-3 was announced, a group at OpenAI posted a paper entitled "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models". Its lead author, Jared Kaplan, was a theoretical physicist: a professor at Johns Hopkins who had worked on quantum gravity and had lately turned his attention to machine learning. Several of his co-authors had trained as physicists too. And the paper is immediately recognisable as the work of physicists. It does not put forward a new architecture or a new technique. It measures things, across many orders of magnitude, and looks for regularities.

Here is what they did. They trained a large number of Transformer language models, of every size from a few hundred weights up to over a billion. They varied the amount of training data, and the amount of computation. And for each one, they recorded how well it predicted text that it had not seen: that is, its cross-entropy loss on a test set, the measure of average surprise that we met in chapter five.

Then they plotted the results on logarithmic axes. And the points fell on straight lines.

A straight line on a log-log plot signifies a power law. Here it means that each time you multiply the size of the model by ten, the loss falls by about the same fixed fraction. The relationship held with considerable precision over six or seven orders of magnitude in model size. A similar power law held for the quantity of training data, and another for the total amount of computation spent on training. As long as none of the three was allowed to become a bottleneck for the others, performance got better smoothly and predictably as each was increased.

There were two more findings, which mattered just as much. First, the details of the architecture made very little difference. Whether the model was made deep and narrow, or shallow and wide, within reason, hardly mattered. What counted was the total number of weights. The model's shape is a second-order effect; its size is the first-order one. That is the bitter lesson, in numbers.

Second, and this is the finding that carried the weight of a prophecy: the lines showed no sign of bending. At the largest sizes that the authors could afford to test, the trend was continuing just as before. Nothing in the data suggested a plateau.

Think about how that looks to a physicist. You have measured a smooth regularity over seven orders of magnitude. You do not have a theory that explains it; nobody had derived these power laws from first principles, and nobody has done so convincingly since. They are more like Kepler's laws than Newton's: an exact description of what occurs, with no accepted account of why. But a regularity that has held for seven orders of magnitude is one that you would be inclined to extrapolate for another two or three. And that extrapolation was a forecast: build a model ten times bigger, train it on proportionately more data, and it will be better by about this much.

From observation to strategy

It is hard to overstate how much this changed the character of the enterprise. Research is ordinarily a gamble. You try an idea, and it may or may not work, and you cannot know in advance. Scaling laws turned one line of research into something closer to an engineering project. If you want a better model, there is a recipe, and it comes with a quantitative forecast of what you will get. The recipe requires no new insight. It requires money.

That is why GPT-3 was built. To train it cost millions of dollars, at a time when that was thought an outlandish sum to spend on one experiment. OpenAI spent it because they had a curve which told them what they would get. And when the model turned up on the curve, just where it was predicted to be, the reasoning was confirmed, and the next and larger bet became easier to make.

It also explains, at least in part, the corporate history of the years that followed. If progress comes from scale, then progress needs capital, in quantities far beyond the reach of a university laboratory or a non-profit living on donations. That is the logic which carried OpenAI from its non-profit beginnings into a partnership with Microsoft, worth one billion dollars and later many more. It is why, when a group of OpenAI's researchers left in 2021 to found a rival laboratory, Anthropic, among them Jared Kaplan and several of his co-authors on the scaling paper, they in turn found themselves raising billions from Amazon and from Google. The scaling laws made the leading edge of this field into a contest that only a handful of organisations could afford to take part in.

You might fairly ask why Google itself did not get there first. Google had invented the Transformer. It had more computers, more data and more researchers than anyone else. Part of the answer seems to be that it had a great deal to lose. A model that makes up facts with complete confidence is an awkward product for a company whose business rests on being a trusted source of answers. Another part is that Google's researchers were spread over hundreds of projects, with no one of them in a position to commandeer the resources for a single enormous bet. A small, focused laboratory with a conviction and a curve could move faster.

Diminishing returns, or the opposite?

Now let me complicate the picture, because the scaling laws are often cited as if they promised more than they do.

First, look at the shape of a power law. Each tenfold increase in scale buys the same fractional reduction in loss, and in fact the exponent is small: ten times the computation buys a reduction in loss of the order of ten or twenty per cent. To get the same improvement again takes another factor of ten. In terms of loss, then, this is a law of steeply diminishing returns. Each step forward costs ten times what the last one did. From that standpoint, the remarkable thing about the past several years is not that the returns were large, but that people were willing to keep paying exponentially more.

Why were they? Because the loss, the quantity that is being measured, is not what anyone actually cares about. What matters is what the model can do, and the relation between the two is very far from linear. A modest reduction in average surprise per token can be the difference between a model that loses the thread of an argument after a paragraph and one that sustains it for a page; between one that writes code which nearly works and one that writes code which works. Small differences in the error rate per step compound over long chains of steps. So a smooth and slowly improving loss curve is quite compatible with abilities that seem to appear abruptly.

There is a celebrated essay in physics, written in 1972 by Philip Anderson, with the title "More Is Different". Its argument is that when you put together a great many simple components, you get behaviour which is qualitatively new and could not readily have been foreseen from the properties of the components: you will not find superconductivity, or the solidity of a table, in the equations for one electron. People in machine learning took up the phrase, and with it the word emergence. GPT-2 could not do three-digit arithmetic, and GPT-3 could. No one added an arithmetic module. It emerged, with scale. How sharp these transitions really are is a matter of dispute; some researchers have argued that the apparent suddenness is partly a product of the way in which performance is scored. But the basic fact is not in dispute. Bigger models can do things that smaller models cannot do at all, and it is hard to say ahead of time which things those will be.

The second complication concerns the balance between the ingredients. Kaplan's paper had suggested that, when you have more computation to spend, you should put most of it into making the model bigger, and rather less into training on more data. In 2022 a team at DeepMind re-did the analysis with greater care, and found that this was a mistake. The best policy, they showed, is to scale up the two together, and as a rough rule, you want something like twenty tokens of training text for each weight in the model. To prove it, they trained a model which they called Chinchilla. It had only a quarter as many weights as DeepMind's previous flagship, but it was trained on four times as much data, and it beat the larger model on nearly every measure for the same computing budget. The implication was that GPT-3, and all the other large models of that time, were too big for the quantity of data that they had been trained on.

This had a significant consequence. If the amount of data has to grow in step with the size of the model, then data becomes a limit. There is only so much high-quality text in existence. The largest models are by now trained on a substantial fraction of all the suitable text that humanity has produced, and you cannot multiply that by ten by spending more money. We shall see in the coming chapters what the laboratories have done about it.

The three ingredients

It is common in the field to say that progress comes from three things: compute, data and algorithms. We have now met all three, and it is worth saying a little more about the first, because it is the one that you will hear discussed most, and it is the most physical. "Compute", by the way, is used as a noun, meaning computational resources, and I shall use it that way.

The compute used to train the largest models has been growing by a factor of about four or five each year, for over a decade. That is very much faster than Moore's law. It comes from three sources, which multiply together. The chips have got better: each generation of Nvidia's processors does more arithmetic per second and per watt than the one before. The laboratories have bought more of them: a frontier model in 2020 might have been trained on some thousands of GPUs, and those of today are trained on hundreds of thousands, in data centres that are specially built for the purpose. And the runs have got longer.

The scale of what is now being built is hard to take in. One high-end AI chip costs about as much as a car. A leading-edge data centre contains hundreds of thousands of them, and consumes as much electrical power as a city: these facilities are specified not by their floor area but in gigawatts. Each of the big technology companies is spending tens of billions of dollars a year on this, and the plans that have been announced run into the hundreds of billions. The main constraints on progress are no longer just ideas, or even money. They include the number of chips that a single company in Taiwan can manufacture, and the rate at which new power stations can be connected to the grid. Nvidia, the company that once made graphics cards for video games, became for a time the most valuable company in the world. And since 2022, the United States government has treated the most advanced of these chips as a strategic asset, and restricted their export to China.

That is what follows from taking a straight line on a log-log plot seriously.

Alongside the growth in hardware, there is algorithmic progress, which is less visible but, by most estimates, of about the same importance. Better training recipes, better choices of data, and many small refinements to the architecture mean that the compute needed to reach a given level of ability has been falling by something like a factor of three a year. A model that needed a supercomputer in 2020 can be matched today by one that runs on a laptop. When researchers speak of "effective compute", they mean the product of the two: the physical computation, multiplied by the gains in algorithmic efficiency. And when you hear them speak of "orders of magnitude", often abbreviated to OOMs, this is what they are counting. Forecasts of where the technology is going are, underneath, generally estimates of how many more orders of magnitude of effective compute are to come, and by when.

The scaling hypothesis

All of this leads up to a question which divides the field more deeply than any other. How far does this go?

The strong position is known as the scaling hypothesis. It holds that the methods we have now, more or less, with neural networks trained by gradient descent on vast amounts of data, are sufficient to reach human-level intelligence and beyond it, if they are simply scaled up far enough. No fundamental new insight is needed. On this view, the difference between GPT-2 and GPT-4 is of the same kind as the difference between GPT-4 and a system that can do the work of a scientist, and we know how to get from one to the other: more orders of magnitude.

The opposing position is that the curves will bend. Its proponents have included some eminent people, among them Yann LeCun, whom we met in chapter three. Their arguments take various forms. These models, they point out, need far more data than a human being does to learn anything: a child learns language from a few tens of millions of words, not from trillions. The models learn only from text, which is a thin and second-hand record of the world. They do not go on learning after they are deployed. Their failures are sometimes so peculiar as to suggest that they lack some basic ingredient of understanding, and are making up for it by sheer memorisation. On this view, scaling up a language model is like building a taller and taller ladder in order to get to the moon. You make measurable progress every year, and you will never arrive.

I shall return to this debate in the last chapter. For the moment I just want you to see what sort of disagreement it is. It is not, for the most part, a disagreement about what the present systems can do. It is a disagreement about extrapolation: about whether a regularity that has held over many orders of magnitude will hold for a few more. Physicists know better than most how hazardous that is. Every power law has a range of validity, and the interesting physics tends to be found where it breaks down. But they also know that there are times when a simple law carries on working far beyond the regime in which anyone had the right to expect it.

What is beyond question is that, as of the early twenty-twenties, the bet had paid off handsomely. There remained one problem. The models that scaling had produced were strange and unwieldy things: brilliant, erratic completers of documents. To turn one of them into something that a hundred million people would want to talk to took a further set of ideas, and that is where part three begins.

The maths in brief

  • Kaplan et al.: with data not limiting, test loss L(N) ≈ (N_c / N)^α_N, with α_N ≈ 0.076 for N non-embedding parameters. Similar laws for dataset size D (α_D ≈ 0.095) and compute C.
  • What that exponent means: 10× the parameters multiplies the loss by 10^−0.076 ≈ 0.84. A 16% reduction per order of magnitude.
  • Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al.): for a fixed compute budget C ∝ N·D, loss is minimised by scaling N and D equally, roughly D ≈ 20 N (tokens per parameter).
  • Training compute for a transformer is roughly 6 · N · D floating-point operations.
  • "Effective compute" = physical compute × algorithmic efficiency gains.
