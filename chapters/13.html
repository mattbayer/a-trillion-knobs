Chapter 13: The Open Questions

A prize for physics

In October 2024, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton, for foundational discoveries that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks. A good many physicists were taken aback. Was this physics?

There is a case that it was. In 1982 Hopfield, a condensed matter physicist, had shown that a network of simple interconnected units could be made to store memories, by treating it just like a magnetic material. Each unit is like an atomic spin, pointing up or down. The connections between them are like the couplings between spins. The network as a whole has an energy, and the stored memories are the low points of the energy landscape; give it a partial or corrupted pattern, and it rolls downhill to the nearest complete memory. Hinton and a colleague then heated the system up, so to speak, by adding random thermal fluctuations, and used the Boltzmann distribution of statistical mechanics to devise a network that could learn. You have seen how these ideas echo through the story: the landscape of the loss, noise as temperature, the softmax as a Boltzmann distribution, diffusion models running a thermodynamic process in reverse.

The day after, half of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of DeepMind, for AlphaFold, a neural network built around the Transformer's attention mechanism, which predicts the three-dimensional structure of a protein from the sequence of its amino acids. That had been an open problem for fifty years.

So within a single week, the technology that this book describes was honoured both as a product of fundamental science, and as an instrument for doing it. That seems to me a suitable place from which to look at where things stand, and at what is still in dispute. For this last chapter I am going to do something a little different. In place of a narrative, I shall take you through the main arguments that are going on among the people who are building these systems. My intention is not to tell you who is right. I do not know, and you should be somewhat wary of anyone who claims to. It is to give you a map, so that when you hear two researchers disagreeing, you can tell what the disagreement is about.

The first debate: will scaling continue to work?

In chapter nine I described the scaling hypothesis: that more compute, more data, and steady refinement of the methods we already have will be enough to carry us to systems that equal or surpass human beings at nearly all intellectual work. A system of that sort is what people mean, roughly, by artificial general intelligence, or AGI. There is no agreed definition of the term, and you will find that a good deal of apparent disagreement about when AGI will arrive is really disagreement about what it is. Some people use it to mean a system that can do any job which can be done at a computer. Others have in mind something that could run a whole research programme or a company. It is generally worth asking.

The case for continued scaling rests on the trends. The compute devoted to training has been growing several-fold each year. Algorithmic efficiency has been improving at a similar rate. The length of task that a model can carry out has been doubling every several months. People who extrapolate these lines conclude that very capable systems are a matter of years away, and not decades, and some of the heads of the leading laboratories have said as much in public: that within a few years, there may be something like a country of geniuses in a data centre.

The case against is that each of the inputs is approaching a limit. Take data. The models have by now read most of the good-quality text that there is, and it cannot be multiplied by ten. Take compute. Growth has been kept up by spending which itself has been growing exponentially, and that cannot go on for many more doublings before it comes up against the size of the world economy, or the capacity of the chip factories, or the supply of electricity. Hence a sceptic may say that what we have been watching is a single great surge, paid for by a wave of investment without precedent, which will inevitably slow down.

The optimist's rejoinder is the one that we saw in chapter eleven. The limit on data applies to imitation. A model that is learning by reinforcement, from tasks whose outcomes can be verified, is generating its own data, as AlphaGo did by playing against itself. And compute applied at the time of use, in the form of longer reasoning, is a further axis that has only just begun to be explored. So the question becomes whether those new sources of progress can take over from the old ones. This is the thing to listen for when you hear researchers talking about synthetic data, about RL environments, and about whether reinforcement learning "generalises": whether a model trained to do well on ten thousand checkable tasks becomes better at tasks in general, or merely at those.

The second debate: is something missing?

The next debate is about whether the present approach, however far it is scaled, lacks something essential. I mentioned several of these criticisms at the end of chapter nine, and I can now fill them in.

The first concerns the efficiency of learning. A human teenager learns to drive a car in some twenty hours of practice. A child acquires language from a quantity of words that is smaller by a factor of many thousands than that on which a language model is trained. Whatever it is that brains are doing, it extracts a great deal more from each item of experience. The models make up for this by having a vastly greater amount of experience, but the critics maintain that the gap points to a real difference of kind.

The best-known proponent of this view is François Chollet, who argues that we have been measuring the wrong thing. Skill at a task, he says, is not intelligence, since skill can be bought with enough training data, as a lookup table can be made to play chess. Intelligence is the efficiency with which you acquire new skills: how well you deal with a situation which you have not been prepared for. He devised a benchmark made up of small visual puzzles, each of a novel kind, which most people can solve in a moment and which for years defeated the largest models. The recent reasoning models have made large inroads into it, and he has responded with harder versions. The argument about what that shows is still going on.

The second criticism is the problem of continual learning, which came up in chapter eleven. A model does not learn on the job. Its weights are frozen at the end of training, and whatever it works out during a task is gone when the context is cleared. Richard Sutton, the author of "The Bitter Lesson", holds that this is a fatal defect. On his view, real intelligence is a matter of an agent with goals, learning continually from the consequences of its own actions in the world. A language model learns from a fixed record of what people have written, has no goals of its own, and is never surprised by the world, because it never makes a prediction about the world which the world then corrects. It is rather ironic that the man whose essay is the charter of the scaling camp should think that large language models are a dead end, and it is a useful reminder that these camps are not as tidy as they look.

The third criticism is that text is not enough. Yann LeCun has argued for years that a system trained on language can never arrive at a real understanding of the physical world, since language is a compressed and highly incomplete description of it. A house cat, he likes to point out, has a better grasp of how objects move and fall than any language model has. His proposal is for systems that learn what he calls world models: internal simulations of how the world develops, learnt mainly from video and other sensory data, by predicting not the next pixel, but abstract representations of what is to come next. It is related to an old observation in robotics, called Moravec's paradox: the things that people find hard, such as chess and calculus, turn out to be relatively easy for machines, and the things that a toddler can do without effort, such as walking across a cluttered room or picking up an unfamiliar object, are extraordinarily hard. Robotics is still a long way behind language, though it too is now being transformed by the same methods.

A word which you will often hear in this connection is "jagged". The abilities of these systems do not make up a smooth frontier, as a person's do, where competence at hard things implies competence at easy ones. The same model that can prove a difficult theorem may fail at a task that a child would find trivial. Whether that jaggedness is a passing artefact, which will be smoothed away by further training, or a sign of some deep difference in the kind of mind that this is, is one way of stating the whole question.

The third debate: what happens if it works?

Suppose that the optimists are right, and that we are within some years of systems which can do most intellectual work. What then? Here there is a debate that is at least as sharp, and it is as much about economics as about technology.

One position derives from a remark made in 1965 by the statistician I. J. Good, who had worked alongside Alan Turing as a wartime codebreaker. Good pointed out that the design of intelligent machines is itself an intellectual activity. So a machine that surpassed human beings in all intellectual activities would be able to design a better machine, and that one a better one again. He called this an intelligence explosion.

The modern version of the argument is specific. The scarcest input to progress in artificial intelligence, apart from compute, is the labour of a few thousand highly skilled researchers. If models become good enough to do the work of those researchers, to write the code, design the experiments and interpret the results, then that labour is no longer scarce. You could run a million copies. Algorithmic progress, which now yields perhaps a threefold gain in efficiency each year, could be speeded up many times over. And since the product of that research would be still more capable AI researchers, the process would feed on itself. People who take this seriously describe scenarios in which the passage from roughly human-level systems to vastly superhuman ones takes a year or two, and they regard the automation of AI research as the crucial threshold. That is the reason why such close attention is paid to how good the models are at programming, and at machine learning research in particular. It is not only a commercial application. It is the application which might close the loop.

The opposing position is that this leaves out the bottlenecks. Research needs experiments as well as ideas, and experiments need compute, which you cannot conjure up by thinking. A million automated researchers who must share the same data centres may not get on much faster than a thousand human ones. More broadly, sceptics argue that the effect of even very capable AI on the world will be limited by all the things that are not intelligence: the pace at which organisations change, at which factories are built, at which clinical trials are run and regulations rewritten. On this view, artificial intelligence is a "normal technology", like electricity or the internet: immensely important, and transformative over decades, but spreading through the economy at the rate at which institutions can absorb it, and not at the rate at which the models improve. Economists add that when one part of a process becomes much cheaper, the value shifts to whichever parts are still slow, so that growth is governed by its narrowest bottleneck.

When you listen to these discussions, it helps to notice that the two sides commonly agree about the technology. They disagree about how far the world is limited by cognition. If you believe that what mainly holds back science and economic growth is a shortage of very clever people thinking hard, you will expect a transformation that is fast and dramatic. If you believe that what mainly holds them back is physical experiment, capital, and the co-ordination of human beings, you will expect one that is gradual.

The fourth debate: what could go wrong?

I touched on the alignment problem in the last chapter. The wider discussion of risk has several separate strands, and it is useful to keep them apart, because people who are worried about one are often dismissive of another.

One strand is misuse: people using capable systems to do harm, by helping to design a pathogen, for example, or by automating cyber-attacks. This is the least speculative concern, and the laboratories test for it before they release a model.

A second is misalignment: the possibility that highly capable systems will pursue goals that differ from those which their makers intended, and will be hard to correct. How seriously you take this turns very much on your answers to the earlier questions. If you think that systems far more capable than ourselves are decades off, and will arrive by degrees, it looks like a problem that can be dealt with as it arises. If you think that they may be a few years off, and that the transition might be rapid, it looks urgent.

A third is the concentration of power. Building these systems requires huge capital. If they come to do a large share of economically valuable work, then whoever controls them, whether it be a few companies or a few governments, would hold a degree of economic and military power for which there is little precedent. That is why the question of who can obtain advanced chips has become a matter of high geopolitics, and why there is such an argument over open-weight models, which spread the capability widely, with all the benefits and all the dangers that this entails.

And there is a fourth strand, which is the simple disruption of ordinary life: what becomes of work, and of education, and of people's sense of their own worth, if machines can do most of what people are paid to do. This is the strand that requires the fewest assumptions, and it gets, perhaps, less attention than it should.

A listener's toolkit

You told me, in effect, that one of your aims was to be able to listen to researchers in conversation, and to follow what they were saying. So let me finish the substantive part of the book with a handful of questions which you can put to any claim that you hear. These are the distinctions on which, in my experience, most of the confusion turns.

First: is this about the weights, or about the context? When somebody says that a model "knows" or "remembers" or "learned" something, ask which they mean. Knowledge in the weights came from training, and is permanent, and fuzzy. Knowledge in the context was supplied at the time of use, and is exact, and temporary. Much loose talk about memory and learning becomes clear at once.

Second: which stage of the pipeline is this about? Pre-training, in which the model takes in knowledge from a vast corpus by prediction. Post-training, in which it is shaped by fine-tuning and by reinforcement learning into something that reasons and behaves in the desired way. Or the scaffold: the tools, the retrieval and the loops which are built around the finished model. An improvement may come from any of the three, and they have very different implications.

Third: is this a claim about capability, or about reliability? That a model can do something, some of the time, on a good day, is one thing. That it can be relied upon to do it is another. Most impressive demonstrations are of the first kind, and most economic value needs the second. The gap between the two is where a great deal of present-day engineering goes on.

Fourth: is this a benchmark, or the real world? Benchmarks are clean, well specified, and at risk of having leaked into the training data. Real work is messy and under-specified, and depends on context that nobody wrote down. A model's score on a test, and its usefulness to you on Tuesday afternoon, are related, but they are not the same.

Fifth: which curve is being extrapolated, and what would cause it to bend? Almost every forecast in this field is a straight line on a logarithmic plot, extended. That is not an objection; some of those lines have held for a very long time. But ask what the inputs are: chips, power, data, money, researchers; and which of them gives out first.

And sixth: is this training, or inference? The economics, the hardware, and the bottlenecks of building a model are different from those of running it. A good deal of talk about compute goes astray by mixing them up.

Knobs

Let me close by going back to where we started.

In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt built a machine whose weights were knobs, turned by small electric motors, and told the press that its descendants would walk, talk, see, write, reproduce themselves and be conscious of their own existence. He was ridiculed for it, and for half a century the ridicule looked justified.

It is worth going through that list again today. Talking and writing: plainly, yes. Seeing: yes. Walking is coming along. Reproducing themselves, if by that we mean designing and training their own successors, is the subject of serious and well-funded effort, as we have just seen. And as for the last item on the list, there are now researchers at leading laboratories whose job it is to consider whether these systems might have experiences that matter morally. Most of them would say that we do not know. It is a remarkable thing that the question can be put with a straight face.

What Rosenblatt had was the right basic idea, and almost none of what was needed to make it work. Setting out what was needed has been the business of this book. A way of assigning credit through many layers: backpropagation. Enough depth to build abstractions out of abstractions, and the plumbing to get gradients through it: the ReLU, normalisation, the residual connection. Data on the scale of the internet, and a training objective that needs no labels: predict what comes next. An architecture that allows every piece of the input to consult every other piece, and that can be run in parallel on hardware originally made for video games: the Transformer. The nerve to make it a thousand times bigger, and a thousand times bigger again, on the strength of a straight line on a logarithmic plot. And then reinforcement, to turn a predictor into something that pursues an end.

None of these ideas is especially complicated. I hope that is one of the things that you take away. You have followed all of them, and there has been nothing in this book more mathematically demanding than the chain rule and the dot product. What is deep about this field is not the difficulty of its concepts. It is that such simple ingredients, in sufficient quantity, should produce what they do. We turn the knobs downhill, many trillions of times, and at the end there is something that can explain quantum mechanics, or write a sonnet, or find the bug in your code. Nobody fully understands why that works. You are now in a position to follow the argument as people try to find out.

A map of positions

  • Scaling optimists (short timelines): current methods plus more compute, RL and engineering suffice. See Amodei's Machines of Loving Grace; Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness; Kokotajlo et al., AI 2027.
  • "Something is missing": Chollet on skill-acquisition efficiency (Dwarkesh interview); Sutton on continual learning from experience (Dwarkesh interview); LeCun on world models (A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence); Karpathy's "decade of agents" (Dwarkesh interview).
  • "Normal technology": Narayanan & Kapoor, AI as Normal Technology.
  • Risk: Hinton's Nobel lecture; Bengio's writing on AI risk; for the sceptical side, LeCun's public talks.

The listener's six questions

  1. Weights or context?
  2. Pre-training, post-training, or scaffold?
  3. Capability or reliability?
  4. Benchmark or real world?
  5. Which curve is being extrapolated, and what bends it?
  6. Training or inference?
