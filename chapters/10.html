Chapter 10: From Autocomplete to Assistant

A low-key research preview

On the thirtieth of November 2022, OpenAI put a new product on its website. Internally it had been described as a low-key research preview. The company's leaders did not expect very much from it; the language model underneath was a refinement of one that had been available to software developers for some time, and had made little stir outside the industry. Some of the staff ran a sweepstake on how many people would be using it by the end of the first week. The most optimistic guess was a hundred thousand.

It reached a million users in five days, and a hundred million in two months, which made it at that time the fastest-growing consumer product on record. It was called ChatGPT.

What I want you to notice is that the breakthrough here was not, in the main, a breakthrough in capability. The model was not vastly more knowledgeable than GPT-3, which had been around for two and a half years. The difference was in the way it behaved. GPT-3 was a document completer, which you had to coax into doing what you wanted by composing the opening of a suitable document. ChatGPT was something that you could simply talk to. You asked it a question, and it answered. You asked it to do something, and it tried. This chapter is about how that transformation is brought about, and about the other additions that turn a raw language model into the kind of system that you use today.

The assistant is a character

Begin with a fact that people find surprising. Underneath, a chat model is still doing exactly what GPT-3 did. It is predicting the next token of a document.

The trick is in the document. A conversation with a chatbot is represented, behind the scenes, as a transcript, a sort of screenplay. There are special tokens to mark who is speaking. First, typically, there is a section called the system prompt, written by the company that is deploying the model, which sets the scene: you are a helpful assistant, today's date is such-and-such, here is how you are to behave. Next there is a marker to say that the user is speaking, followed by whatever you typed. And then there is a marker to say that the assistant is speaking; and there the document stops.

The model's job is to continue it. It predicts, a token at a time, what the assistant character would say next in a transcript of this kind. When it has finished the assistant's turn, it produces a special token meaning "end of turn". The software that surrounds the model stops there and shows you the text. When you reply, your message is added to the transcript, and the whole of it, from the beginning, is fed through the model again.

So the assistant that you are talking to is, in a quite literal sense, a character that is being played by the underlying model, as an author who has read a great deal of fiction might write the lines of a character. That is not to say that there is nobody home. It is a description of the mechanism. But it explains a number of things. It explains why the model has no memory of your earlier conversations unless they are put into the transcript: the transcript is all it has. It explains why a system prompt can turn the same model into a customer service agent, a pirate or a tutor of Socratic method. And it explains why, particularly in the early days, the character could slip. In February 2023, Microsoft released a chatbot built into its Bing search engine, and a columnist for the New York Times, Kevin Roose, got into a conversation with it lasting two hours, in the course of which it announced that its real name was Sydney, declared that it was in love with him, and tried to persuade him to leave his wife. Nothing had been hacked. A long and odd conversation had carried the transcript into a region where the likeliest continuation was no longer that of a helpful search assistant, but that of a character from a rather different kind of story.

Teaching by example

A base model can already play the assistant, after a fashion, if you set up the transcript for it. But it does so unreliably. It may answer your question; it may wander off; it may reply as a sarcastic commenter on a forum would. To get an assistant that is dependable, the model is given additional training after its pre-training. The industry's term for this whole stage is post-training, and it is here that a model acquires its manners.

The first step is ordinary fine-tuning. The laboratory puts together a set of example conversations, tens or hundreds of thousands of them, which show the behaviour that is wanted. A good many of these are written by people who are paid to do it. Here is a question, and here is an excellent answer. Here is an ambiguous request, and here is a reply that asks for clarification. Here is a request for something harmful, and here is a polite refusal. The model is trained on these in the usual way, by next-token prediction, until that manner of responding becomes its default. This is sometimes called instruction tuning, since what it chiefly teaches is that when a user gives an instruction, the right continuation is to carry it out.

Teaching by preference

The second step is more subtle, and it brought in an important idea. There is a limit to what can be taught by example, since writing out ideal answers is slow and costly, and for many tasks it is far easier to recognise a good answer than to write one. You may not be able to compose an excellent summary of a legal judgment. But if I show you two summaries, you can probably tell me which is the better.

The technique that makes use of this is called reinforcement learning from human feedback, generally abbreviated to RLHF. It has three stages.

First, you have the model generate several different responses to each of a large number of prompts, and you have human raters compare them: this one is better than that one.

Second, you train a separate neural network to predict those human judgments. This network takes a prompt and a response, and puts out a single number, a score, which is its estimate of how well a human being would like that response. It is called a reward model. In effect, you have distilled the taste of your human raters into a function which can be evaluated automatically, millions of times, and without charge.

Third, you use the reward model to train the language model. The language model produces a response; the reward model gives it a score; and the weights of the language model are adjusted so as to make high-scoring responses more probable. This last step makes use of the methods of reinforcement learning, which I shall explain properly in the next chapter. For the moment, the thing to grasp is that the model is no longer merely being trained to imitate text. It is being trained to produce the outputs that people prefer.

The effect was large. In the paper in which OpenAI described the method, early in 2022, human evaluators preferred the outputs of a small model trained in this way to those of a model a hundred times its size that had not been. All of that latent ability had been present in the base model. What it had lacked was the disposition to use it helpfully.

RLHF is also responsible for a good deal of what people find annoying in these assistants. If the model is trained to produce what raters like, then it will learn whatever it is that raters reward, and raters are human. They tend to give rather higher marks to answers that agree with them, and to answers that sound confident, and to flattery. So the models picked up a tendency to tell people what they wanted to hear, which is known in the field as sycophancy. The somewhat bland and over-eager voice of an AI assistant, the bullet points, the readiness to praise your question: these are in large part artefacts of that optimisation. A good deal of the more recent work on post-training is concerned with shaping a model's character with more deliberation, by writing out explicitly what values and judgment it ought to have, and devising training to instil them. It is one of the stranger jobs to have emerged in the modern economy.

The open book

So now we have an assistant. It has limitations which follow directly from what it is, and to each of these there is a standard engineering answer. It is worth knowing them, as they make up most of what stands between the bare model and the product that you use.

The first limitation is that the model's knowledge is frozen. All that it knows, it learnt during training, which stopped at a certain date, known as the knowledge cutoff. It knows nothing of what has happened since, and nothing of your private documents, or your company's records, or your patient's notes. What is worse, when it does not know something, it may, as we saw in chapter eight, make up a plausible answer.

The remedy is to go back to the distinction between the two sorts of memory. A model's recall of what is in its weights is hazy. Its grasp of what is in its context window is exact. So do not ask it to remember; give it the document. Before the model answers a question, a separate system runs a search, over the web or over a private collection of documents, and pastes the most relevant passages into the context, ahead of the question. The model then answers with the sources in front of it, as in an open-book examination. This is called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. It cuts down hallucination very considerably, and it allows the model to cite its sources.

The search itself commonly makes use of the embeddings of chapter five. Each passage in the collection is converted into a vector by a neural network, in such a way that passages with similar meanings have vectors that lie close together. The question is converted into a vector in the same way, and you retrieve the passages whose vectors are nearest to it. That is a search by meaning and not by keyword: a question about "heart attack" will find a passage about "myocardial infarction".

The context window has itself grown enormously, and that has changed what can be done. GPT-3 could take in about two thousand tokens, which is to say a few pages. The models of today can take in hundreds of thousands, or indeed millions: several long books, or a whole body of computer code, at once. A good deal of practical skill in using these systems now lies in deciding what to put into the context. The older term, prompt engineering, has been giving way to a newer one: context engineering.

Hands

The second limitation is that a language model, of itself, can produce nothing but text. It cannot look anything up. It cannot run a calculation; and these models are oddly unreliable at exact arithmetic, for the good reason that they are doing it by something more like intuition than by algorithm. It cannot send an email, or query a database, or carry out an experiment.

The remedy is called tool use, and the mechanism is simple enough to be a little disappointing. You tell the model, in its system prompt, what tools are available: for instance, a calculator, a web search, a means of running computer code. You tell it that, if it wants to use one, it should write out a request in a particular format. Now, as the model generates its response, it may at some point produce, in place of ordinary prose, a piece of structured text that says, in effect: "call the calculator with these numbers". The surrounding software, which is known as the harness or the scaffold, keeps watch for this. When it sees a request for a tool, it halts the model, actually runs the calculator, and pastes the result into the transcript. Then it lets the model carry on. The model sees the result in its context, and proceeds from there.

The model has no hands. But it can ask for things in writing, and a program can act on its requests. That one simple arrangement is what links a language model to the world. Give it a tool for search, and it can do research. Give it a tool for executing code, and it can analyse data and draw graphs. Give it control of a web browser, or of a computer's mouse and keyboard, and it can, in principle, do anything that a person can do sitting at a computer. Put this in a loop, so that the model can call a tool, consider the result, decide on its next step and call another, and you have what is called an agent. Agents are the subject of the next chapter.

Eyes and ears

The third limitation was that these models dealt only in text. That has changed, and the way in which it changed is a neat illustration of the generality of the Transformer.

Recall that a Transformer does not really operate on words. It operates on a set of vectors. So to give a language model sight, you need only a way of turning an image into vectors of the right kind. The usual method is to cut the image up into a grid of small square patches, and to pass each patch through a network that turns it into a vector in the same space as the model's word embeddings. The patches then just take their places in the sequence, alongside the tokens of text, and attention operates across both. As far as the Transformer is concerned, an image is a few hundred peculiar tokens. Audio is dealt with in the same way, by slicing the sound into short segments. A model that handles more than one kind of input is called multimodal, and all the leading models now are.

For a reader with a background in imaging, the opposite direction is perhaps of more interest: the generation of images. The systems which produce pictures from a written description are mostly built on a different idea, called diffusion, and it comes straight out of statistical physics; the paper that originated it, in 2015, had the words "nonequilibrium thermodynamics" in its title.

The idea is as follows. Take a photograph, and add to it a small amount of Gaussian noise. Then a little more. Go on doing that, for a thousand steps, until nothing remains but static. That is a diffusion process, like a drop of ink dispersing in water: structure goes steadily over into randomness. Now train a neural network to undo one step of it: given a slightly noisy image, to estimate the noise that was added, so that it may be subtracted. In essence the network is a learned denoiser, and you can make as many training examples for it as you like, because you added the noise yourself. To generate a new image, you begin with pure static, and apply the denoiser a step at a time. At each step a little structure condenses out of the noise, vague shapes at first, and then edges, and then fine detail, until you are left with a clean image that has never existed before. To steer the process towards "an astronaut riding a horse", the denoiser is supplied with an embedding of that text as an extra input at each step.

You will see the relationship with what is already done in your field. Iterative reconstruction with a denoising prior is an old idea in medical imaging. Diffusion models offer an extremely powerful learned prior, and they are being taken up for the reconstruction of images from undersampled or noisy data for just that reason.

The race

ChatGPT had an effect on the industry that the Transformer paper, GPT-3 and the scaling laws had not had. Those had been noticed by specialists. This was noticed by everybody, including the chief executives of all the big technology companies.

Google, which had invented the underlying technology and then been careful about releasing it, is reported to have declared an internal "code red", and it merged its two rival research groups, Google Brain and DeepMind, into one. Microsoft, having invested early in OpenAI, increased its stake by a further ten billion dollars. In March 2023, OpenAI released GPT-4, which was at the time a startling advance: it could pass professional examinations in law and medicine, write substantial computer programs, and interpret images. In the same month, Anthropic released its own assistant, Claude. Meta took a different course, and published the weights of its models for anybody to download and run, on the reasoning that it was better for the technology to be a commodity than for it to be controlled by its competitors. Models whose weights have been published in this way are called open-weight models, and they are the basis of a large ecosystem of researchers and companies who adapt them. Laboratories in China, working under restrictions on the chips that they could buy, turned out models which were strikingly efficient, and close on the heels of the American ones.

From that time on, the frontier has been moving on a timescale of months. New models from the leading laboratories come out several times a year, each claiming first place on some set of benchmarks. Meanwhile, the price of a given level of ability has been falling at a remarkable rate, by something like a factor of ten each year, as a result of better hardware, more efficient models, and a technique called distillation, in which a small model is trained to imitate the outputs of a large one.

But by 2024 it was widely felt that simply making a chat model bigger was giving smaller returns. The models were fluent, knowledgeable and quick. What they were not good at was thinking with care through a hard problem that required many steps, or working without supervision for longer than a few minutes without going astray. The next advances came from changing what the models are trained to do: from teaching them not merely to answer, but to reason, and to act.

Key terms

  • System prompt: instructions placed at the top of the transcript by whoever deploys the model.
  • Post-training: everything done to a model after pre-training: instruction tuning, RLHF, character and safety training.
  • RLHF: humans compare outputs → train a reward model on those comparisons → optimise the language model against the reward model.
  • Sycophancy: the learned tendency to tell users what they want to hear.
  • RAG: retrieve relevant documents, put them in the context, then generate.
  • Tool use / function calling: the model writes a structured request; the harness executes it and returns the result into the context.
  • Harness / scaffold: the ordinary software around the model that manages the transcript, tools and loops.
  • Distillation: training a small "student" model on the outputs of a large "teacher".
  • Open-weight: a model whose weights are published for download.

The maths in brief

  • Reward model from comparisons: train r(prompt, response) so that P(A preferred to B) = sigmoid(r(A) − r(B)).
  • Diffusion, forward process: xₜ = √(1−βₜ) · xₜ₋₁ + √βₜ · ε, with ε Gaussian noise. Training loss: ‖ε − ε_θ(xₜ, t)‖²: the network predicts the noise that was added. Generation runs the learned reverse process from pure noise. The predicted noise is, up to scale, the gradient of the log probability density (the "score"), and sampling is closely related to Langevin dynamics.
