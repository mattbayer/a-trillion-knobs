Chapter 1: The Function Machine

Before we begin

This book was written to be listened to. That shapes it in a few ways. There are no equations read aloud, no tables, and no moments where I ask you to look at a figure. When an idea really wants a picture or a formula, I will describe it in words, and the picture itself lives on the companion website, where each chapter has a page of notes, the mathematics written out properly, links to the original papers, and a handful of interactive demonstrations you can play with when you are next at a screen.

The book has three parts. Part one is about mechanics: what a neural network is, how it learns, and why it has layers. Part two is about language: how we got from networks that recognise pictures of cats to the transformer, and from the transformer to machines that write. Part three is about the modern stack: how a raw language model becomes an assistant, how it is taught to reason and act, what we know about what goes on inside it, and what the people building these systems are arguing about right now.

I am assuming you are comfortable with the ideas of a function, a derivative, a vector and a matrix, and that you have at some point fitted a curve to data. That is all you will need. If you have a background in physics or engineering, you will find that much of this field feels oddly familiar, and I will point out the family resemblances as we go.

Let us start in the summer of 1958.

The machine that would be conscious

In July of 1958, the United States Navy held a press conference in Washington to show off a new kind of computer program. It ran on an IBM 704, a machine the size of a room, and what it did was not, on its face, very impressive. It was shown punch cards, some marked on the left and some marked on the right. At first it guessed at random which was which. After about fifty cards it had taught itself to tell them apart.

The man behind it was Frank Rosenblatt, a twenty-nine-year-old research psychologist at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory in Buffalo. He called his creation the perceptron. And Rosenblatt was not shy about what it meant. The New York Times reported the next day that the Navy had revealed the embryo of a computer that it expected would one day walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its own existence.

That did not happen. Within about a decade, the perceptron was a discredited idea, its funding was drying up, and the phrase "neural network" was on its way to becoming something you avoided putting in a grant application. Rosenblatt died in a boating accident in 1971, on his forty-third birthday, before he could see any of it vindicated.

And yet. The systems that today write software, pass medical licensing exams and hold conversations are direct descendants of that card-sorting program. Not metaphorical descendants. The basic unit Rosenblatt used is still the basic unit. The basic idea, that you do not program the machine but instead let it adjust itself in response to examples, is still the basic idea. What changed between 1958 and now is mostly a matter of how many units, how they are wired together, how they are adjusted, and how much data and computation is poured in. This book is the story of those changes.

But first we need to be clear about what the thing actually is.

A function with knobs

Here is the least mystical description of a neural network I can give you. A neural network is a mathematical function with a very large number of adjustable parameters.

If you have a physics background, you have been doing a small version of this your whole career. Suppose you measure the extension of a spring under different loads. You plot the points, and you fit a straight line. A straight line is a family of functions with two adjustable parameters, the slope and the intercept. Fitting means finding the particular slope and intercept that make the line pass as close as possible to your data. Once you have them, you have a model: give it a new load, and it predicts an extension.

Notice the structure of what you did. First, you chose a family of functions, in this case straight lines. Second, you defined what "close" means, probably the sum of the squared distances between the line and the points. Third, you had a procedure for finding the best parameters. For a straight line there is a neat formula. And at the end, all of the knowledge extracted from the data lives in two numbers.

A neural network is exactly this, with three differences.

The first difference is scale. Instead of two parameters, there are millions, or billions, or by now trillions. The network known as GPT-3, released in 2020, had one hundred and seventy-five billion. Each one is just a number, like the slope of your line.

The second difference is the family of functions. In physics, you usually choose the form of the function from theory. You fit a straight line because Hooke's law says it should be a straight line. You fit an exponential because you believe in radioactive decay. The parameters mean something: a spring constant, a half-life. In a neural network, the family of functions is not chosen because theory says the answer has that form. It is chosen because it is enormously flexible, so flexible that it can approximate nearly any function you might care about, and because it turns out to be possible to fit. The individual parameters do not mean anything you could name. The millionth parameter is not the anything constant. This is a real philosophical shift, and it is worth sitting with. It is modelling without a theory of the thing being modelled.

The third difference is the fitting procedure. There is no neat formula. Instead there is a process of repeated small adjustment, which is called training, and which is the subject of the next chapter.

Those parameters have a name you will hear constantly. They are called the weights. When someone says that a company has released the weights of a model, or that a lab is worried about its weights being stolen, this is what they mean: the list of numbers that the fitting procedure produced. A modern language model, stripped of everything else, is a file containing some hundreds of billions of numbers, plus a comparatively tiny piece of code, a few hundred lines, that says how to combine them with an input to produce an output. The code is public knowledge. The numbers are the crown jewels. They are what hundreds of millions of dollars' worth of electricity and chips were spent to find.

So that is the top-level picture. A function, with knobs, fitted to data. Now let us open the box and look at what kind of function.

The neuron

The building block is called a neuron, by loose analogy to the cells in your brain, though I would encourage you not to take the analogy too seriously. An artificial neuron is a very simple little calculation.

It takes in some numbers; call them the inputs. Each input has its own weight. The neuron multiplies each input by its weight, and adds up the results. Then it adds one more number, called the bias, which does not depend on the inputs at all. Finally, it passes that total through a simple fixed function, called the activation function, and the result is the neuron's output. That is the whole thing. Multiply, add, squash.

Let me make it concrete. Imagine a neuron whose job is to decide whether a small patch of an X-ray image contains a vertical edge. Say the patch is three pixels by three, so there are nine inputs, each one the brightness of a pixel. Give the three pixels in the left column weights of minus one. Give the three pixels in the right column weights of plus one. Give the middle column weights of zero. Now think about what the weighted sum does. If the patch is uniformly bright, or uniformly dark, the left and right columns cancel, and the sum is zero. If the patch is dark on the left and bright on the right, the sum is large and positive. The neuron responds strongly to exactly one kind of pattern, a dark-to-bright vertical edge, and the pattern it responds to is written directly in its weights.

That is the best first intuition for what weights are. A neuron's weights are a template. The weighted sum measures how well the input matches the template. In the language of linear algebra, it is a dot product between the input vector and the weight vector, and a dot product is a measure of alignment.

The bias sets the threshold: how good does the match have to be before the neuron cares? And the activation function turns the raw score into a response. In the earliest neurons, the activation function was a hard step: output one if the sum is above zero, and zero otherwise. The neuron fires or it does not. Later designs used smooth S-shaped curves, and the modern favourite is almost embarrassingly simple: if the sum is positive, pass it through unchanged, and if it is negative, output zero. That one is called the rectified linear unit, or ReLU, and it will make an important appearance in chapter four.

Why have an activation function at all? Why not just output the weighted sum? Hold that question. The answer turns out to be the key to why networks have layers, and we will get to it in chapter three.

There is a geometric way to see a neuron that I find helpful. Think of the inputs as coordinates of a point in space. With two inputs, it is a point on a plane. With nine inputs, it is a point in nine-dimensional space, which you cannot picture, but the mathematics does not care. The equation "weighted sum plus bias equals zero" defines a flat surface cutting through that space: a line in two dimensions, a plane in three, a hyperplane in general. On one side of it the neuron is on, and on the other side it is off. The weights set the orientation of that dividing surface, and the bias slides it back and forth. So a single neuron is a device for cutting space in two with a flat cut.

Logic, learning and Rosenblatt's trick

The idea of the artificial neuron dates to 1943, to a paper by an unlikely pair. Warren McCulloch was a neurophysiologist in his mid-forties. Walter Pitts was a homeless teenage runaway and self-taught logician of astonishing gifts. Together they showed that networks of simple threshold units, units that fire if enough of their inputs are active, could compute any logical function. You can build AND, OR and NOT from such units, and from those you can build anything. It was an existence proof that something brain-like could, in principle, compute.

But in the McCulloch and Pitts picture, somebody has to set the weights by hand. The network is designed, not taught. Rosenblatt's great contribution, fifteen years later, was a procedure for learning the weights from examples.

His procedure is simple enough to state in a breath. Show the perceptron an example. If it gets the answer right, do nothing. If it says "no" when the answer was "yes", then nudge each weight a little in the direction of the input, so that next time the weighted sum for that input will be higher. If it says "yes" when the answer was "no", nudge the weights the other way. Repeat, over and over, across all your examples.

In the geometric picture, each mistake tilts the dividing surface slightly toward getting that example right. And Rosenblatt and others proved a lovely theorem: if there exists any flat cut that separates the yes examples from the no examples, this procedure is guaranteed to find one in a finite number of steps.

Rosenblatt then built the thing in hardware. The Mark One Perceptron had a camera made from a grid of four hundred photocells, twenty by twenty. Its weights were physical: they were variable resistors, potentiometers, and when the machine learned, small electric motors turned the knobs. I love this detail, because it makes the abstraction literal. The weights really were knobs. Learning really was the turning of knobs. Everything since has been the same activity carried on by other means.

The limits of a single cut

So what went wrong?

The trouble is in that phrase, "if there exists a flat cut". A single neuron can only divide the world with one flat surface. Many problems cannot be solved that way, and the standard example is almost insultingly small. It is called exclusive-or, or XOR. You have two inputs, each either zero or one. The answer should be "yes" if exactly one of the inputs is one, and "no" if they are both zero or both one.

Picture the four possible inputs as the four corners of a square. The two "yes" cases sit at one pair of diagonally opposite corners. The two "no" cases sit at the other pair. Now try to draw a single straight line that puts both yes corners on one side and both no corners on the other. You cannot do it. It is geometrically impossible. And so a perceptron, no matter how long you train it, can never learn this trivially simple function.

In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert of MIT published a book, titled simply Perceptrons, which worked out these limitations with mathematical rigour. Minsky and Rosenblatt had known each other since they were teenagers, having attended the same high school in the Bronx a year apart, and they had spent much of the sixties sparring at conferences. The book was elegant and correct, and it landed on a field that had made promises it could not keep.

Now, the remedy for the XOR problem was known, even then. You use more than one layer. Have two neurons each make their own flat cut, and then have a third neuron look at the outputs of the first two and combine them. With two cuts you can carve out a band across the middle of the square, and the problem is solved. Minsky and Papert knew this perfectly well.

The difficulty was the learning rule. Rosenblatt's procedure works for a single layer because, when the output is wrong, you know exactly which weights to blame: the ones connected to the output. But when there is a layer of neurons in the middle, hidden between input and output, what should those neurons have done? Nobody tells you the right answer for a hidden neuron. If the final output is wrong, was it the fault of the last neuron, or of the middle ones that fed it bad information, and if so which, and by how much? This puzzle has a name: the credit assignment problem. In 1969 nobody had a practical solution to it, and Minsky and Papert wrote that they saw little reason to expect that one would be found.

The effect, combined with the general deflation of over-hyped artificial intelligence research at the time, was chilling. Funding moved elsewhere. Mainstream artificial intelligence spent the next two decades largely on a different programme, sometimes called symbolic AI, in which intelligence was to be achieved by writing down rules and logic explicitly, by hand. This period for neural networks is known as the first AI winter. A small band of researchers kept the idea alive, mostly at the margins, and we will meet several of them.

Where this leaves us

Let me gather up the vocabulary, because these words will carry through the rest of the book.

A model is a function with adjustable parameters. The parameters of a neural network are its weights, plus the biases, though people often say "weights" to mean all of them. A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and applies an activation function. A network is many neurons wired together, with the outputs of some feeding the inputs of others. Training is the process of adjusting the weights so that the network's outputs match examples. Inference is what happens afterwards, when the weights are frozen and you simply run the function on new inputs. When you type a question into a chatbot, you are doing inference. Nothing in the model changes as a result.

And we have two ideas that are worth holding on to. First, the weights of a neuron act as a template, and the neuron measures how well its input matches. Second, a single neuron can only make one flat cut through the space of its inputs, which is not enough, and the cure is layers.

But layers come with the credit assignment problem. If a network has a million weights buried in its middle, and its output is wrong, how do you work out which way to turn each of the million knobs?

The answer turned out to be something you learned in your first calculus course. It is the chain rule. Turning the chain rule into the engine that drives all of modern artificial intelligence is the subject of the next chapter.

The maths in brief

  • A neuron computes y = f(w·x + b), where x is the input vector, w the weight vector, b the bias, and f the activation function.
  • Step activation: f(z) = 1 if z > 0 else 0. ReLU: f(z) = max(0, z). Sigmoid: f(z) = 1 / (1 + e^-z).
  • Perceptron learning rule: on a mistake, w ← w + η (target − output) x, with a small step size η.
  • The decision boundary is the hyperplane w·x + b = 0. w is its normal vector.
