Chapter 6: The Memory Problem

Networks with a loop

The networks we have met so far take in an input of fixed size, a picture of so many pixels, and produce an output. Information flows in one direction, from bottom to top, and then the computation is over. Language does not come like that. A sentence can be three words long or three hundred. It arrives in order, and the order matters. "Dog bites man" is not news; "man bites dog" is.

The natural way to process a sequence is the way you are processing this one: one item at a time, keeping a running memory of what has come before. The network design built on that idea is called the recurrent neural network, or RNN. The idea goes back to the nineteen-eighties.

Here is how it works. The network maintains a vector called the hidden state. Think of it as the network's working memory, a few hundred numbers that summarise everything that it has read so far. At each step, the network takes two things: the embedding of the next word, and its own hidden state from the previous step. It combines them, through the usual matrix multiplication and non-linearity, and produces a new hidden state. Then it goes on to the next word and does the same again, with the same weights. Read a word, update the memory. Read a word, update the memory. When the end of the sentence is reached, the final hidden state is, in principle, a summary of all of it. And at any point, you can attach an output layer to the hidden state and ask it to predict the next word.

It is an appealing design. It handles sequences of any length. It builds in a sensible inductive bias: the same rules apply at each position in the sequence, in much the same way as a convolution applies the same filter at each position in an image. And there is at least a passing resemblance to what it feels like to read.

Forgetting

In practice, plain recurrent networks did not work well, and you already have the tools to see why.

How do you train one? You use backpropagation, as always. But consider the path that the error signal must take. Suppose the network makes a mistake at the fiftieth word of a sentence, and the information that it needed was at the third word. To correct the weights, the error signal has to travel backwards from step fifty to step three, through forty-seven successive applications of the same update. In effect, a recurrent network processing a fifty-word sentence is a network that is fifty layers deep, with one layer for each time step.

And we know what happens to gradients in very deep networks. They vanish. The signal is multiplied by a similar factor at each step back, and it shrinks exponentially. It was precisely this setting that Sepp Hochreiter was analysing in the 1991 thesis I mentioned in chapter three. The practical upshot is that a plain RNN can learn connections between words that are five or ten steps apart, and little beyond that. It forgets. The start of a long sentence has faded from its memory by the time it reaches the end, and worse, it cannot learn to remember, because no training signal reaches back that far.

Hochreiter did not just diagnose the illness. In 1997, with his supervisor Jürgen Schmidhuber, he published a cure. They called it long short-term memory, or LSTM. The name is odd, but the meaning is: a short-term memory that lasts for a long time.

The insight was to change the way that memory gets updated. In a plain RNN, the entire hidden state is rewritten at each step; it is pushed through a matrix and a squashing function, and comes out scrambled. In an LSTM there is a separate channel, called the cell state, that runs along the whole sequence like a conveyor belt. By default, what is on the belt just stays there, carried from each step to the next without being altered. The network changes it only by means of small, deliberate, additive edits, and these are controlled by components called gates. A gate is a little sub-network that outputs numbers between zero and one, which serve as dimmer switches. One gate decides what to erase from the belt. One decides what new information to write to it. One decides how much of it to reveal to the rest of the network at this step. And all of the gates are learned.

You may spot the family resemblance. Because the memory is carried forward by addition, and is not repeatedly transformed, gradients can flow back along the conveyor belt across many time steps without vanishing. It is the same trick as the residual connection from chapter four, an express lane for gradients, and the LSTM had it eighteen years earlier. Schmidhuber, I should mention, has been arguing for decades that he and his students have received too little credit for this and several other ideas, and on this particular point he is clearly right.

Like so much else, the LSTM lay almost unused for a decade and more, awaiting data and computing power. When those arrived, it took over. By around 2015, LSTMs were behind the speech recognition in your phone, the predictive text on your keyboard, and a growing share of machine translation. That year, a doctoral student at Stanford named Andrej Karpathy wrote a widely read blog post titled "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks", in which he trained LSTMs to predict text one character at a time. Fed on Shakespeare, the network turned out passable mock-Shakespeare, with speaker names and stage directions. Fed on the source code of the Linux operating system, it wrote things that looked like computer code, with correctly matched brackets and plausible comments. The output was nonsense, but it was nonsense with a structure, and it gave many people their first inkling that a network trained to do nothing but predict the next symbol could pick up a surprising amount about the form of what it was reading.

Translation as a test case

The problem that drove the next breakthroughs was machine translation. It was a good problem: commercially valuable, hard, and well supplied with data, since the proceedings of the European and Canadian parliaments and the documents of the United Nations furnished millions of sentences, each carefully translated by professionals.

The dominant approach in 2014 was called statistical machine translation. Systems of this kind were prodigious feats of engineering: pipelines of many separate components, tables of phrases and their translations, n-gram language models, rules for reordering words, each one built and tuned by large teams over years. Google Translate was such a system.

That year, a group at Google that included Ilya Sutskever, lately arrived from Toronto, proposed to replace the whole pipeline with one neural network. Their paper was called "Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks", and the design was almost provocatively simple. Take two LSTMs. The first, called the encoder, reads the English sentence one word at a time. When it gets to the end, its hidden state is a single vector, a few thousand numbers, which is supposed to capture the meaning of the entire sentence. Hand that vector over to the second LSTM, called the decoder, as its initial memory. The decoder then produces the French translation one word at a time, each word conditioned on that vector and on the words it has written so far.

So: a whole sentence is squeezed down into one vector, and a whole sentence is unpacked from it. No phrase tables, no linguistic rules, no alignment of words. Just pairs of sentences, a loss function, and gradient descent. With a big enough network, it matched the carefully engineered systems. In a talk he gave at the time, Sutskever stated his creed with a bluntness that people still quote: if you have a large dataset, and you train a very big neural network, then success is guaranteed.

The bottleneck

The design had an obvious weak point, and you have probably seen it already. Everything that the decoder knows about the source sentence has to pass through that one vector. It is a bottleneck. A short sentence may fit into it. A fifty-word sentence with three subordinate clauses will not, and indeed, the quality of the translations dropped off sharply as the sentences grew longer. A computational linguist named Ray Mooney expressed the general scepticism in a line that became famous, which I shall paraphrase for politeness: you cannot cram the meaning of a whole sentence into a single vector.

Think about how a human translator works. You do not read a long sentence, close the book, and then write the translation from memory. You keep the original in front of you, and as you write each word of the translation, your eyes go back to the part of the source that is relevant at that moment.

Towards the end of 2014, a paper appeared that gave the network the ability to do just this. It came out of Yoshua Bengio's lab in Montreal, and its first author, Dzmitry Bahdanau, was an intern there. The mechanism it introduced was given the name attention.

Attention

Here is the idea. Let the encoder read the source sentence as before; but do not throw away its intermediate states. Keep all of them: one vector for every word of the source, each representing that word in its context. Now, when the decoder is about to produce the next word of the translation, let it look back over all of those source vectors and decide which of them matter at this moment.

Specifically, the decoder takes its own current state, which you can think of as expressing "this is what I am trying to write next", and compares it with each of the source vectors in turn. Each comparison yields a score: how relevant is that source word to what I am doing now? The scores are passed through a softmax, the same function from the last chapter, which turns them into a set of positive weights that add up to one. Then the decoder takes a weighted average of the source vectors, using those weights. Source words with high scores contribute a lot to the average, and those with low scores contribute almost nothing. The result is a single vector, a custom-made summary of the source sentence, tailored to the needs of this particular step. For the next word of output, the decoder asks again, and gets a different summary.

So the fixed bottleneck has gone. In its place the network has something like a spotlight, which it can move across the source sentence, to light up whichever part it needs.

Three things about this mechanism are worth dwelling on, because everything that follows is built on them.

The first is that it is soft. The network does not pick out one source word to look at. It takes a blend of all of them, in which some count for much more than others. That matters a great deal, for a technical reason. A hard choice, look at word three and ignore the rest, is not differentiable; you cannot take the gradient of a decision. A weighted average is a smooth function of its weights, and the weights are smooth functions of the scores. So gradients flow through the whole thing, and the network can learn where to look by ordinary backpropagation. Nobody tells it which source words go with which target words. It works that out, because looking in the right place reduces the loss.

The second is that attention is content-based. The network retrieves information according to what it is, not according to where it is. The decoder does not say "give me the fourth word". It says, in effect, "give me whatever is relevant to what I am writing now", and it finds it wherever in the sentence it happens to be. If you have a computing background, think of it as a soft version of a database lookup. There is a query, which is what I am looking for. There are keys, which describe what each stored item has to offer. And there are values, the items themselves. An ordinary lookup returns the one value whose key matches the query exactly. Attention returns a blend of all the values, each weighted by how well its key matches the query. Query, key and value: hold on to those three words, because in the next chapter they become the central machinery.

The third is that distance no longer matters. In a recurrent network, information from the start of a sentence has to survive a journey through every intervening step to be of use at the end. With attention, the decoder can reach directly to any position in the source in a single hop. The first word is no further away than the last.

And as a bonus, you can see what the network is doing. Plot the attention weights as a grid, with source words along one side and target words along the other, and you get a picture of which source words the network was looking at as it wrote each target word. For English and French, the bright cells run mostly along the diagonal, since the two languages have a similar word order. But where French puts the adjective after the noun, as in "zone économique européenne" for "European economic area", the bright cells neatly reverse themselves. The network had found out how the words of the two languages line up with one another, and no one had told it to.

The great awakening

The effect on translation was immediate. Within two years, attention-equipped neural networks had overtaken the statistical systems that had been refined over the previous two decades.

In the autumn of 2016, Google replaced the system behind Google Translate with a neural one: deep stacks of LSTMs, an encoder and a decoder, connected by attention. The switch was first made, without any announcement, for translation between Japanese and English, and some users in Japan noticed overnight that the translations had gone from stilted to fluent, and wondered what had happened. By Google's own measurements, the new system reduced translation errors by more than half for several pairs of languages. It was a bigger improvement in a single step than the old system had made in the whole of the previous decade. A long article about the project in the New York Times Magazine at the end of that year was entitled "The Great A.I. Awakening".

It also said something about the economics of research. The old system had been the work of hundreds of engineers and linguists over many years. The new one was built by a much smaller group in about nine months, and its code was a fraction of the length. All of that accumulated linguistic engineering had been replaced by one generic learning machine and a mountain of data. This pattern has a name, which we will come to in chapter nine.

What was still wrong

So by the end of 2016, the state of the art for language was the LSTM with attention. It worked. But the people who were building these systems were up against a limit, and it was not a limit of accuracy. It was a limit of speed.

A recurrent network is sequential by nature. To compute the hidden state at word fifty, you need the hidden state at word forty-nine, and for that you need word forty-eight. There is no way of doing the steps out of order, and no way of doing them at the same time. And this is exactly what a GPU is bad at. A GPU gets its power from doing thousands of things at once. Give it a long chain of calculations, each of which has to wait for the one before, and most of that vast parallel machine sits idle.

Compare that with the convolutional networks that had triumphed in vision. A convolution applies the same filter at every position of an image independently, so all the positions can be computed simultaneously. Vision networks could soak up as much parallel hardware as you could give them. Language networks could not. And since, as we have seen, the route to better performance was bigger networks and more data, that was a serious problem. Training was taking weeks. The architecture was holding back the scale.

A few people at Google began to ask a question that sounds, with hindsight, like the obvious one. In these systems, the LSTM was there to carry information along the sequence, and attention had been bolted on to help. But attention could already carry information between any two positions directly, and it could do so for all positions at once. Was the recurrent part really doing anything essential? What would happen if you took it out, and built a network from nothing but attention?

The paper that answered that question was published in June 2017. Its title gave the answer away. It was called "Attention Is All You Need".

The maths in brief

  • RNN update: hₜ = tanh(W_h hₜ₋₁ + W_x xₜ + b). The same W at every step, so backprop through T steps multiplies by (roughly) W_hᵀ T times.
  • LSTM cell state: cₜ = fₜ ⊙ cₜ₋₁ + iₜ ⊙ c̃ₜ, where the forget gate fₜ and input gate iₜ are in (0,1). Additive, like a residual connection.
  • Bahdanau attention at decoder step t: scores eₜⱼ = score(sₜ, hⱼ) for each source position j; weights αₜⱼ = softmax over j of eₜⱼ; context cₜ = Σⱼ αₜⱼ hⱼ.
