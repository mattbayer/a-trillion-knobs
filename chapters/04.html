Chapter 4: The ImageNet Moment

Two missing ingredients

At the end of the last chapter, the believers in neural networks had their ideas in place, and lacked two things: data, and computing power. This chapter is about how each arrived, from directions nobody in the field had planned, and what happened when they met.

Start with the data. In 2006, a young professor named Fei-Fei Li had an idea that most of her colleagues thought was a poor use of a promising career. Computer vision research at the time was focused on algorithms: cleverer features, better classifiers. The datasets used to test them were small, a few thousand images in a few dozen categories. Li suspected the field had its priorities backwards. A child learns to see by looking at an enormous variety of things, for years. Perhaps what the algorithms lacked was not cleverness but experience.

So she set out to build a dataset on the scale of the visual world: every category of object for which English has a noun, with hundreds or thousands of example photographs of each. The plan called for labelling millions of images by hand. Her first estimate, using undergraduates, was that it would take around ninety years. What rescued the project was a then-new service from Amazon called Mechanical Turk, which let her farm out tiny labelling tasks to tens of thousands of people around the world, for pennies each. By 2009, the dataset, which she called ImageNet, held millions of labelled images in thousands of categories. She presented it at the leading vision conference that year, where it was relegated to a poster session. Few people saw the point.

To attract attention, Li and her colleagues started an annual competition. Teams were given about 1.2 million training images drawn from a thousand categories, among them a hundred and twenty different breeds of dog, and their systems were then scored on images they had not seen. The standard measure was the top-five error rate: the system is allowed five guesses, and it is marked wrong if none of them is right. In 2010 the winning entry got about twenty-eight per cent wrong. In 2011, about twenty-six. Progress was real, but incremental, and all the top entries were built on hand-engineered features.

Chips made for games

The second ingredient came from video games.

Drawing a three-dimensional scene on a screen, sixty times a second, means doing the same simple arithmetic on millions of pixels and triangles at once. Through the nineteen-nineties and two-thousands, a competitive industry grew up building chips specialised for exactly that: graphics processing units, or GPUs. Where an ordinary processor has a handful of powerful cores that do complicated things one after another, a GPU has thousands of simple ones that all do the same thing at the same time.

Now think about what a neural network needs. The forward pass is a matrix multiplication followed by a simple function applied to every element. The backward pass is the same. Matrix multiplication is the perfect parallel job: every entry of the result can be computed independently of every other. The arithmetic of neural networks and the arithmetic of video games turn out to be nearly the same arithmetic.

The company that saw furthest was Nvidia. In 2007, at the insistence of its chief executive, Jensen Huang, Nvidia released a software system called CUDA, which let programmers use its graphics cards for general scientific computing. It was a costly bet on a market that barely existed, and for years investors grumbled about it. By around 2009, a few neural network researchers had noticed that a pair of gaming cards costing a thousand dollars could train their networks ten to fifty times faster than a conventional processor. A factor of fifty turns an experiment that would take a year into one that takes a week. It is the difference between a thing being impossible in practice and being a student project.

The bedroom in Toronto

Which brings us to two graduate students in Geoffrey Hinton's lab in Toronto.

Alex Krizhevsky was quiet, and an exceptionally gifted programmer, with a knack for coaxing performance out of GPUs. Ilya Sutskever was intense and visionary, and had an unshakeable belief that neural networks, made large enough and given enough data, would simply work. Both had been born in the Soviet Union and come to Canada as children. It was Sutskever who looked at ImageNet and saw that it was the experiment the field had been waiting for: a dataset big enough to feed a truly large network. He talked Krizhevsky into taking it on.

Krizhevsky wrote the code for a convolutional network, far larger than anything LeCun had built, and trained it on two Nvidia gaming cards, which sat in a computer in his bedroom at his parents' house. Training took the better part of a week for each attempt. The network had eight layers, about six hundred and fifty thousand neurons, and sixty million weights.

In the autumn of 2012, the results of that year's ImageNet competition were announced. The second-best entry, a refined version of the conventional approach, had a top-five error rate of about twenty-six per cent, about the same as the previous year's winner. The Toronto network, which became known as AlexNet, scored fifteen per cent.

In a field where a one-point improvement was a good year, that was not an incremental advance. It was a discontinuity. An approach that most of the vision community had written off had not merely won; it had made everything else look obsolete. People who were in the room when the results were presented describe a mixture of excitement and something close to dismay. Within two years, essentially every entry in the competition was a deep convolutional network, and the feature engineers had either converted or retired from the field. By 2015 the winning error rate was under four per cent, better than the roughly five per cent that a well-trained human manages on the same test.

What made it work

AlexNet was, conceptually, LeCun's network from 1989, made bigger. But it did include a few new tricks that made bigness possible, and they are worth understanding, because they show you the kind of thing that counts as progress in this field.

The most important was the activation function. Recall the vanishing gradient problem: the sigmoid function's slope is small, so that the error signal shrinks as it travels back through each layer. AlexNet used the rectified linear unit instead, the ReLU, which had been championed a couple of years earlier by Hinton's group and by Bengio's. The ReLU outputs zero if its input is negative, and passes the input through unchanged if it is positive. It looks too simple to be a serious non-linearity: it is just a ramp with a kink at zero. But on the positive side its slope is exactly one. Gradients pass back through it without shrinking at all. Networks using it trained several times faster, and could be made deeper. That a change this trivial could matter this much tells you something about how unexplored the territory was.

The second trick was aimed at overfitting, and it is called dropout. During training, each time an example is processed, you randomly switch off half of the neurons, a different random half each time. This sounds like sabotage. But it prevents the network from relying too heavily on any one neuron, or on fragile conspiracies among particular neurons, and forces it to spread knowledge redundantly. Hinton has said the idea came to him at his bank, where he noticed that the tellers kept being rotated, and realised that this would make it hard for any group of them to collude to defraud the bank.

The third was simply to manufacture more data. Take each training image and make shifted copies, mirrored copies, copies with slightly altered colours. A mirrored cat is still a cat, and the network learns from all of them. This is called data augmentation.

And the fourth was the GPU, without which none of the rest would have mattered.

The auction

The aftermath is one of the stranger episodes in the history of technology. Hinton, Krizhevsky and Sutskever formed a company. It had no products and no plans for any; its assets were the three of them. In December 2012, during the field's main annual conference, held that year at a casino hotel on the shore of Lake Tahoe, Hinton auctioned the company off by email from his hotel room. Four bidders took part: Google, Microsoft, the Chinese search company Baidu, and a small London start-up called DeepMind. The bidding went up in million-dollar increments. When it reached forty-four million dollars, Hinton stopped the auction and sold to Google, which is what he had wanted to do all along.

That auction was the starting gun for a talent war. Facebook hired Yann LeCun to found an artificial intelligence laboratory. Google bought DeepMind, for a reported sum of more than half a billion dollars. Graduate students who knew how to train neural networks, of whom there were at that point perhaps a few hundred in the world, found themselves being offered the salaries of professional athletes. Research that had lived in a handful of university labs moved, within about three years, into the largest and richest corporations on earth. That shift, in who does the research and who pays for it, has shaped everything since.

Going deeper

After 2012, a kind of gold rush set in to make networks deeper, and it soon ran into a wall. ReLU had eased the vanishing gradient problem, but it had not removed it. Beyond about twenty layers, something perverse happened: deeper networks did worse than shallower ones, and not just on test data, but on their own training data. That could not be overfitting. It was a failure of optimisation. The deeper network could, in principle, have copied the shallower one and left its extra layers doing nothing; but gradient descent could not find even that solution.

A handful of innovations between 2014 and 2016 knocked the wall down. Each of them is now part of the standard kit, and all of them are inside every large language model, so let me go through them.

The first was better optimisers; Adam, which I mentioned in chapter two, dates from 2014. The second was careful initialisation: choosing the scale of the initial random weights so that signals neither grow nor shrink, on average, as they pass through each layer. This is an essentially physical argument about keeping the variance of a signal constant through a chain of amplifiers.

The third was normalisation. If you worry that the numbers flowing through the network will drift to be far too large or too small, then just force them not to: at each layer, rescale the activations to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. The first version of this, called batch normalisation, came out of Google in 2015, and it made deep networks dramatically easier to train. A later variant, layer normalisation, is the one used in transformers.

The fourth, and the one I most want you to remember, was the residual connection. It came in late 2015 from a team at Microsoft's research laboratory in Beijing, led by Kaiming He. The idea is this. Ordinarily, each layer takes its input and transforms it into something new, which replaces the input. In a residual network, each layer instead computes a correction, which is then added to its input. The output is the input, plus a learned adjustment.

Why should that help? Think about the path of the gradient on its way back. In an ordinary network, it has to pass through every layer's transformation, getting distorted at each one. With residual connections, there is an express lane: the addition passes the gradient straight back, unchanged, around each layer. Signals can travel from the output all the way to the earliest layers without ever being squeezed. And think about what a layer has to do if it has nothing useful to contribute. In an ordinary network it would have to learn to reproduce its input exactly, which is surprisingly hard. In a residual network it just outputs zero.

If you have ever integrated a differential equation numerically, a residual network will look familiar. Each layer says: the new state is the old state plus a small increment that depends on the old state. That is one step of Euler's method. A residual network is, in a sense, a system evolving through time, with depth playing the role of time, and that analogy has been made mathematically precise.

With residual connections, He's team trained a network a hundred and fifty-two layers deep, and won the 2015 ImageNet competition. Soon afterwards people trained networks of a thousand layers, simply to show that it could be done. The depth barrier was gone. Their paper went on to become one of the most cited scientific papers of the century, in any field. When we open up the transformer in chapter seven, you will find residual connections running through it like a spine.

What are architects actually designing?

This is a good moment to step back and answer a question you may have been forming. When researchers design a network architecture, what is it that they are designing? What are the choices, and what are they trying to achieve? I think there are four things.

The first is inductive bias: building the right assumptions into the wiring. Convolution assumes that images are local and translation-symmetric. We will see other architectures that build in assumptions about sequences. The art lies in choosing assumptions that are true of your data, and general enough not to get in the way.

The second is trainability. An architecture is not merely a family of functions; it is a family of functions that gradient descent must be able to search. Many of the important innovations, the ReLU, normalisation, residual connections, do nothing to change what the network can represent in principle. They change how easily gradients flow through it. A large part of architecture design is plumbing for gradients.

The third is computational efficiency: getting the most learning for each unit of computation, on the hardware that actually exists. An idea that cannot be expressed as big matrix multiplications on a GPU is, in practice, an idea that will lose to one that can. Hardware and architectures have evolved together.

And the fourth is not architecture at all, but it matters at least as much: the choice of what data to train on and what loss to minimise. What question, exactly, are you asking the network to get good at? In part two we will see that the right choice of question turned out to be the most powerful lever of all.

I should also be honest about the method by which these designs are found. There is very little theory that can tell you ahead of time whether an architecture will work. People have intuitions; they try things; they keep what helps. The field has a wry name for this, a play on gradient descent: graduate student descent.

The puzzle of generalisation

Before we leave part one, I owe you a return to the elephant. Why do these enormous networks not overfit?

The puzzle was put sharply in a 2016 paper with the provocative title "Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization". The authors took a standard image dataset and replaced all the labels with random ones, so that there was nothing to learn; the picture of a cat might be labelled "aeroplane". They then trained a standard network on this garbage. The network achieved a perfect score on the training data. It simply memorised a million arbitrary labels. So these networks certainly have the capacity to memorise anything at all. And yet, trained on real labels, the very same network does not just memorise; it finds patterns that hold on new images. Classical statistical theory says that a model which is capable of fitting random noise cannot be trusted to generalise. Here was one that did.

A related finding is called double descent. The textbook picture says that as you make a model bigger, its error on new data first falls, as it becomes able to capture the real pattern, and then rises, as it starts to fit the noise. And that is what you see, up to a point. But keep going, past the point where the model is just big enough to fit the training data perfectly, and the test error turns round and starts to fall again, often to below its previous best. The textbook picture is only the left-hand half of the true curve.

The best current understanding runs something like this. When a model has far more parameters than it needs, there is not one setting of the weights that fits the training data, but a vast continuum of them. Some are wild, wiggly functions that generalise terribly. Others are smooth and simple. So the question becomes which one training picks. And it appears that gradient descent, starting from small random weights, has a built-in preference for the simple ones. It is not that the network could not overfit. It is that the training procedure quietly favours solutions that do not. This preference is called implicit regularisation, and it is not written anywhere in the code. It is an emergent property of the dynamics. A physicist might say that the simple solutions occupy overwhelmingly more of the volume of weight space, so that a noisy search finds them for much the same reason that a gas fills its container.

This is an active area of research, and it is not a closed question. But the practical lesson was absorbed quickly, and it overturned the instincts of a generation of statisticians. Bigger models are not more dangerous. Bigger models are better. Hold on to that thought, because by the time we reach chapter nine it will have been turned into an industrial strategy.

The end of the beginning

In the four years after AlexNet, deep learning swept through one field after another. Speech recognition went first; the error rates of the voice recognition on your phone fell by a quarter almost overnight. Then image recognition, then medical image analysis, then machine translation. In March 2016, a system built by DeepMind, called AlphaGo, defeated Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players in the world, at the game of Go, a feat that most experts had believed to be at least a decade away.

But one domain was proving stubborn, and it was the one that mattered most. Language. Networks could label a photograph and transcribe a spoken sentence, but they could not really read, or write, or hold up their end of a conversation. The architectures that conquered vision were not suited to it. Language is a sequence; its meaning depends on order, and on connections between words that may be far apart. To handle it, the field needed a different way of turning words into numbers, and a different kind of network.

That is the story of part two.

The maths in brief

  • ReLU: f(z) = max(0, z); f′(z) = 1 for z > 0, so backward signals are not attenuated.
  • Dropout: during training multiply each activation by a random 0/1 mask; at test time use all units (with rescaling).
  • Normalisation: x̂ = (x − μ) / σ, followed by a learned scale and shift. Batch norm takes μ, σ over the mini-batch; layer norm takes them over the features of a single example.
  • Residual block: x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l). Compare forward Euler: x(t+Δt) = x(t) + Δt·g(x(t)).
  • Backward through a residual block: ∂L/∂x_l = ∂L/∂x_{l+1} · (1 + ∂F/∂x_l). The 1 is the express lane.
