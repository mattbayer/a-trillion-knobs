Chapter 3: Why Depth?

A question left hanging

In chapter one I left a question hanging. A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs and then passes it through an activation function, a little non-linear squashing step. Why bother with the squashing? Why not just pass along the weighted sum?

The answer is that without it, layers would be pointless. Suppose every neuron simply output its weighted sum. A layer of such neurons takes a vector of inputs and produces a vector of outputs, each a weighted sum of the inputs. That is a linear transformation; in other words, it is multiplication by a matrix. Now stack a second layer on top. That is another matrix multiplication. But a matrix times a matrix is just another matrix. Two linear layers are exactly equivalent to one linear layer with different weights, and so are ten, and so are a thousand. You can stack linear operations as high as you like, and you never get anything richer than a single one. You are still making flat cuts.

The non-linearity is what breaks this collapse. Put a bend between each pair of matrix multiplications, and the stack can no longer be simplified. Each layer can now do something that the layers before it could not. So the recipe for a neural network is an alternation: linear, bend, linear, bend. A matrix multiplication to mix the information, a simple non-linear function applied to each number separately, and repeat. Almost every network in this book, including the giant language models, is a variation on that alternation.

A network built in the plainest way, with each neuron in one layer connected to every neuron in the next, is called a multi-layer perceptron, or MLP. The layers between input and output are the hidden layers. The number of layers is the depth of the network, and the number of neurons in a layer is its width. "Deep learning" simply means learning with networks of many layers. The name is a description of the geometry, not a claim about profundity.

Layers as changes of coordinates

So what do the layers do? The best way I know to think about it is this: each layer re-describes its input in a new coordinate system, one in which the problem is a little easier.

Go back to the XOR problem. Four points at the corners of a square, with the "yes" answers on one diagonal and the "no" answers on the other, and no single straight line that can separate them. A two-layer network solves it like this. The hidden layer has two neurons. One of them learns to fire when at least one input is on. The other learns to fire only when both inputs are on. Now forget the original inputs, and describe each of the four cases by what those two hidden neurons are doing. In that new description, in those new coordinates, the four points have moved. The "yes" cases are the ones where the first hidden neuron is on and the second is off. And that condition can be checked with a single flat cut. The output neuron makes that cut, and the problem is solved.

The hidden layer did not solve the problem. It transformed the problem into one that a simple linear cut could solve. This is the general principle. The last layer of nearly every network is doing something very simple, essentially drawing flat surfaces. All the layers before it are there to bend, stretch and fold the space of inputs until the things you want to tell apart are separable by flat surfaces.

Picture two categories of data tangled together like two crumpled sheets of paper, one red and one blue, wadded up in a ball. No flat cut will separate the red from the blue. Each layer of the network is one move in un-crumpling the ball: a stretch here, a fold there. After enough moves, the red sheet is on one side and the blue on the other, and a single cut divides them. On the companion site, there is a demo that lets you watch a small network do precisely this to points on a plane, and I would encourage you to try it, because it makes the idea visceral in a way that words cannot.

The vector of neuron outputs at a given layer has a name, which you will hear constantly. It is called a representation: the network's internal description of the input at that stage of processing. People also call these vectors activations, or sometimes embeddings. The claim of deep learning, the thing asserted in the title of that 1986 paper, is that backpropagation discovers useful representations by itself. You specify only the input and the desired output. The intermediate descriptions, the coordinates in which the problem becomes easy, are found by the training process.

To appreciate how radical that was, you have to know how things were done before. If you wanted a computer to recognise objects in images in the year 2005, you did not feed it pixels. You first computed features, designed by clever humans: measures of edge orientations, of texture, of corner-ness, with names like SIFT and HOG. You fed those hand-crafted features to a comparatively simple learning algorithm. The intelligence was in the feature design, and a doctoral thesis might be devoted to a better feature. This was called feature engineering, and it was most of the work. Deep learning proposed to make it obsolete, by learning the features and the classifier together, end to end, from raw data. For those of you in medical imaging, this is the transition from hand-built radiomic features and tuned filter pipelines to networks that take the raw image in at one end.

If one layer is enough, why use many?

Here is a fact that seems, at first, to undercut the whole enterprise. In 1989, a mathematician named George Cybenko proved what is now called the universal approximation theorem. It says that a network with just a single hidden layer can approximate any reasonable function as closely as you like, provided the hidden layer is wide enough.

So depth is not strictly needed. One hidden layer can, in principle, do everything. Why, then, do modern networks have dozens or hundreds of layers?

The catch is in the words "wide enough". The theorem tells you that a solution exists. It does not tell you how many neurons it needs, and for many functions of interest, the answer is an astronomically large number. A single hidden layer approximates a function the way a lookup table does, by devoting a few neurons to each little region of the input space. That works, but the number of regions grows exponentially with the number of input dimensions.

Depth offers an escape, because depth allows reuse. Think about how you would write a computer program. You could, in principle, write any program as one gigantic flat table listing the right output for every possible input. Nobody does that. You write small functions, and then functions that call those functions, and then functions that call those. Each level builds on the one below, and a small amount of code yields an enormous range of behaviour. A deep network can do the same thing. A feature computed in layer two can be used by a hundred different features in layer three, each of which can be used by a hundred in layer four. The number of distinct patterns the network can express grows exponentially with depth, while the number of weights grows only in proportion. There are functions that a deep network can represent with thousands of neurons, and that a shallow network would need more neurons than there are atoms in the universe to match.

Now, that only helps if the functions we care about have that kind of structure: wholes made from parts, made from smaller parts. And here is the remarkable thing. The world does seem to be like that. An image of a face is made of eyes and a nose and a mouth. An eye is made of curves and patches of texture. A curve is made of short edges. An edge is a particular arrangement of a few pixels. Language is the same: letters, words, phrases, sentences, arguments. Physical law is local and compositional, and the structure of the things in the world reflects that. Deep networks work, in this view, because their architecture mirrors the hierarchical structure of the data. A physicist might be reminded of the renormalisation group, in which you describe a system at successively coarser scales, each built from the one below. The analogy is loose, but people have taken it seriously.

And this is not just a story. When researchers trained deep networks on images and then went looking at what the individual neurons responded to, they found exactly this hierarchy. Neurons in the first layer respond to edges at particular orientations and to blobs of colour. In the next layers, to corners, and textures, and simple patterns. Further up, to parts of objects: eyes, wheels, fur, text. And near the top, to whole objects. Nobody designed that. It emerged from gradient descent.

Seeing like a cat

That hierarchy had been found once before, in a different kind of network. In 1959, two neurophysiologists at Johns Hopkins, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel, were recording from single neurons in the visual cortex of an anaesthetised cat, while projecting patterns onto a screen in front of it. They found cells that responded not to spots of light, but to edges: a bar of light at one particular angle, in one particular place. Other cells, a stage further along, responded to an edge at a given angle anywhere within a larger region. They called these simple cells and complex cells, and they proposed that the visual system is built as a hierarchy, with each stage assembling more elaborate features from the simpler ones before it. The work won them a Nobel Prize.

The person who turned that insight into a trainable machine was a Frenchman named Yann LeCun. LeCun had come across neural networks as an engineering student in Paris in the early nineteen-eighties, when hardly anyone in France was working on them, and had independently worked out a version of backpropagation. He spent a year as a postdoctoral researcher with Geoffrey Hinton in Toronto, and in 1988 he joined Bell Labs in New Jersey, at that time perhaps the greatest industrial research laboratory in the world. There he set out to get a network to read handwritten digits; specifically, the zip codes on envelopes, from data provided by the United States Postal Service.

His design is called a convolutional neural network, and if you work in imaging, you already understand most of it.

Think about the edge-detecting neuron from chapter one: nine weights, arranged three by three, looking at one small patch of an image. A plain, fully connected network would need a separate such neuron, with its own separately learned weights, for every position in the image. That is wasteful, and worse, it ignores something we know perfectly well about images: an edge is an edge, wherever it happens to be. So LeCun's network uses the same nine weights at every position. You slide the little template across the whole image, and at each location record how well the image matches. What you get is a new image, a map showing where that feature occurs.

You will recognise this operation. It is a convolution of the image with a small kernel. It is the same thing you do when you apply a Sobel filter to find edges, or a Gaussian to smooth. The one difference, and it is the whole point, is that the numbers in the kernel are not designed by an engineer. They are weights, learned by backpropagation like any others. A convolutional layer learns a bank of such filters, a few dozen or a few hundred, each producing its own feature map. The next layer then applies learned filters to those maps, finding patterns among the patterns. In between, the maps are typically shrunk, by a step called pooling, so that higher layers see larger regions of the image at coarser resolution. Edges, then combinations of edges, then combinations of those: Hubel and Wiesel's hierarchy, made out of learned convolutions.

This design builds two assumptions about images into the wiring of the network. One is locality: nearby pixels are related, and features are built from small neighbourhoods. The other is translation symmetry: a feature is the same thing wherever it appears. Assumptions of this kind have a name which is very much worth knowing. They are called inductive biases. An inductive bias is knowledge about the problem that is built into the architecture, as opposed to being learned from the data. A convolutional network does not need to learn that a cat in the top left is the same as a cat in the bottom right, because it is incapable of believing otherwise. And because the same few weights are reused everywhere, it needs vastly fewer parameters and far less data than a fully connected network would.

Remember the idea of inductive bias. A lot of the history that follows can be read as an argument about how much of it you want. More built-in structure means learning faster from less data. But it also means the network can only learn what its builders thought to permit. We will see that argument come to a head in chapter nine.

LeCun's network worked. By the late nineteen-nineties, a system based on it was reading the handwritten amounts on a substantial fraction of all the cheques deposited in the United States. It was a real, deployed, commercially valuable neural network. And the wider field of artificial intelligence, by and large, shrugged.

The second winter

Why the shrug? Because, outside of a few niches like digit recognition, neural networks in the nineteen-nineties did not work very well, and there was a good reason.

Convolutional networks for digits were only a few layers deep. When people tried to train properly deep networks, with ten layers or twenty, training failed. The network just would not learn. The cause was identified in 1991 by a German student named Sepp Hochreiter, in a diploma thesis that was written in German and went largely unread for years. It is called the vanishing gradient problem.

Recall how backpropagation works. The error signal is passed backwards through the network, layer by layer, and at each layer it gets multiplied by a factor that depends on the weights and on the slope of the activation function. With the S-shaped sigmoid function that everybody used, that slope is never more than a quarter, and is usually much less. So at each layer, on its way back, the signal shrinks. Go back through ten layers, and you are multiplying ten small numbers together. The gradient decays exponentially with depth. By the time it reaches the early layers, it is indistinguishable from zero, and those layers, receiving no signal about which way to move, do not learn. Occasionally the factors are larger than one, and then the opposite happens: the gradient grows exponentially and training blows up.

So the very thing that makes deep networks powerful, the long chain of composition, made them untrainable. Depth was a fine theory which nobody could use.

Meanwhile, rival methods were flourishing. The most successful was the support vector machine, developed by Vladimir Vapnik, who, as it happens, worked down the corridor from LeCun at Bell Labs. Support vector machines came with something neural networks lacked: mathematical guarantees. The optimisation problem was convex, a single bowl, with one minimum that you were certain to find. There was a rigorous theory of how well the result would generalise. They performed as well as neural networks or better on the benchmarks of the day. To a serious-minded researcher around the year 2000, neural networks looked like alchemy: a bag of unprincipled tricks for a non-convex problem, with too many knobs and no theory. Papers that used them were routinely rejected from the main conferences, sometimes without much of a reading. This was the second winter.

Three people, above all, refused to give up. Geoffrey Hinton, by then at the University of Toronto. Yann LeCun, who left industry for New York University. And Yoshua Bengio, at the University of Montreal, who had overlapped with LeCun at Bell Labs. In 2004, Hinton persuaded a Canadian research foundation, known as CIFAR, to fund a small programme that would bring together the scattered believers for regular workshops. It was modest money, but it kept a community alive. They began, quite deliberately, to use a new name for their work, one that carried none of the baggage of the old. They called it deep learning.

In 2006, Hinton published a method for training deep networks one layer at a time, which sidestepped the vanishing gradient problem and showed that depth really did pay off. The particular technique was soon superseded and is no longer used. But it rekindled interest, and it sent a generation of graduate students to Toronto, Montreal and New York. In 2018, Hinton, LeCun and Bengio would share the Turing Award, computing's highest honour, for work that for most of their careers had been regarded as a dead end.

What was missing

Looking back from here, what is striking about this period is how much was already in place. By 1990, the field had the neuron. It had backpropagation and gradient descent. It had multi-layer networks, and the understanding that hidden layers learn representations. It had convolutional networks. Nearly every conceptual ingredient of the revolution that was coming already existed, a quarter of a century before it arrived.

What was missing was not ideas. It was two rather more mundane things: data, and computing power. The networks of the nineties were trained on thousands of examples, on computers a million times slower than the ones we have now. Their creators believed, on not much evidence, that the same methods would do extraordinary things if only they could be scaled up. Most of their colleagues believed that if a method does not work at small scale, there is no reason to think it will work at large scale, which is, to be fair, usually true.

In 2012, the believers got their experiment. The result was so decisive that it ended the argument more or less overnight, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

The maths in brief

  • A layer: h = f(W x + b). W is a matrix with one row of weights per neuron. A deep network is the composition f(W₃ f(W₂ f(W₁ x + b₁) + b₂) + b₃).
  • Without f: W₂(W₁ x) = (W₂W₁) x, a single linear map. Depth collapses.
  • Convolutional layer: h[i,j] = f( Σ_{u,v} K[u,v] · x[i+u, j+v] + b ), with the same learned kernel K at every position (i,j).
  • Vanishing gradient: the backward signal through n layers scales like a product of n terms Wᵀ·f′(z). For the sigmoid, f′ ≤ 0.25.
