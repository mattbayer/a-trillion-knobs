Chapter 7: Attention Is All You Need

Eight authors

The most influential paper in the modern history of artificial intelligence was written by eight people at Google, in a few hectic months in the first half of 2017. Its first page carries a footnote which says that all eight contributed equally and that the order of their names is random. That is unusual, and it gives you a sense of how the thing came about: not as the plan of one senior scientist, but as a collaboration that snowballed, picking up people in corridors.

The seed was planted by Jakob Uszkoreit, a researcher whose father happened to be a well-known computational linguist. Uszkoreit had become convinced that the attention mechanism might be enough by itself to handle language, without any recurrent network underneath, and he had a name ready for a model that would transform its representations step by step. He would call it the Transformer. Two colleagues, Ashish Vaswani and Illia Polosukhin, joined him in trying to make it work. Niki Parmar and Llion Jones came aboard, and then Łukasz Kaiser, and his intern Aidan Gomez, who was twenty years old and still an undergraduate student at the University of Toronto.

The early versions worked, but did not beat the LSTMs. What turned the corner, by all accounts, was the arrival of Noam Shazeer. Shazeer was something of a legend inside Google; he had joined the company in 2000 and built, among other things, the system that asks "did you mean" when you misspell a search. He overheard a conversation about the project, decided that it was the most promising thing he had come across in years, and rewrote the whole code base. His version was faster and cleaner, and then the team started taking parts out to see what would break. Remarkably little did. The simpler they made the model, the better it performed.

The title was Llion Jones's contribution, a nod to the Beatles song "All You Need Is Love". The paper was posted in June 2017, and presented that December at the field's main conference. It reported that the Transformer had set a new record for the quality of machine translation between English and German, and between English and French. And it had got there with a small fraction of the training cost of its rivals: three and a half days, on eight GPUs.

Nobody involved thought they had found a route to machines that could pass the bar exam. It was a paper about translation. Within a few years, all eight authors had left Google, most of them to found companies, and the architecture they described had become the foundation of very nearly everything.

Let us take it apart. I am going to describe the version that is used in modern language models, which is a little simpler than the original, and I will describe it in more detail than anything else in this book, because once you have this, you have the central object of the field.

The setting

Start with what goes in and what comes out. What goes in is a sequence of tokens: the text so far. What comes out is a probability distribution for the token that comes next.

The first step is the one from chapter five. Each token is looked up in the embedding table and replaced by its vector. So if the text is a thousand tokens long, we now have a thousand vectors, one sitting at each position. In a large modern model, each of those vectors might be a list of several thousand numbers.

Here is the mental picture I would like you to hold. Imagine those thousand vectors as a thousand columns standing side by side, one for each token. The network consists of a stack of layers, and as we move up through the layers, each column's vector gets progressively revised. At the bottom, the vector at a given position just represents the token there, on its own, out of context: the word "bank", in all its ambiguity. By the time we reach the top, that vector has been enriched with information from all over the text. It now represents that token in its context, and more than that, it contains whatever is needed to predict what follows.

Each layer does two things to these columns, one after the other. First, it lets the columns exchange information with each other. That is attention. Second, it lets each column, separately, think about what it has gathered. That is an ordinary multi-layer perceptron. Communicate, then compute. Then the next layer does it all again. That is the whole of the architecture. The rest is details, though some of the details are clever.

Self-attention

In the last chapter, attention was a bridge between two networks: the decoder attended to the encoder's states. The Transformer uses the same mechanism for a different purpose. It lets the positions in a single sequence attend to one another. This is called self-attention, and it is how the columns communicate.

Remember the vocabulary of query, key and value. In self-attention, every position plays all three roles at once.

Take a particular position: the word "it", in the sentence "the trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big". The vector at that position is multiplied by a matrix of learned weights to produce a new, smaller vector: its query. You can think of the query as a broadcast question. For a pronoun, the question might be something like: "I am looking for a noun, singular, a thing and not a person, mentioned earlier".

Meanwhile, the vector at each position is multiplied by a second learned matrix, to give that position's key. The key is a sort of advertisement: this is the kind of information I have to offer. The key at the position of "trophy" might say, in effect: "singular noun, physical object, subject of the sentence".

Now compare the query from "it" with the key from every position. The comparison is a dot product, the same measure of alignment that we met in the very first chapter. Where the query and the key point in similar directions, the dot product is large: this position has what I want. Where they are unrelated, it is close to zero. That gives a score for every position in the sequence. Put the scores through a softmax, and they become a set of weights that sum to one. Perhaps sixty per cent on "trophy", thirty on "suitcase", and scraps on everything else.

Finally, each position's vector is multiplied by a third learned matrix, to produce its value. The value is the actual content that it will hand over if it is attended to. Take the average of all the values, weighted by the attention weights. That weighted average is the answer to the query. And it is added to the vector at the position of "it". The column for "it" now contains some trophy-ness, which it did not contain before.

That is one attention operation, for one position. The same thing happens at every position simultaneously. Every token sends out a query, every token advertises a key, every token offers a value, and each one's vector is updated with a blend of the others'.

I have dramatised it by putting words in the mouths of the queries and the keys, and I should be clear that this is a cartoon. Nobody writes those questions and advertisements. A query is just a vector, the product of a learned matrix and the vector in the column. The three matrices that produce queries, keys and values begin as random numbers, and are shaped by gradient descent like every other weight. All that training cares about is that the predictions at the end come out right. Whatever questions it is useful for the tokens to put to one another, the network will learn to ask them.

But here is what I want you to appreciate about this mechanism. In an ordinary neural network layer, the pattern of connections is fixed: this neuron is wired to those neurons, with these weights, and that is that, whatever the input. In attention, the pattern of who talks to whom is computed afresh for each input. The wiring depends on the data. For one sentence, position ten draws on position three; for another, it draws on position seven. It is this dynamic, content-dependent routing of information that gives the Transformer its flexibility.

Many heads

One round of attention lets each token ask one kind of question. But there are many relationships in a sentence that matter at once. A verb may need to find its subject, and also its object, and also the adverb that modifies it. A pronoun needs its antecedent. A closing bracket needs its opening bracket.

So the Transformer runs several attention operations side by side, within each layer. Each has its own three matrices, and so its own sort of query, key and value. These are called attention heads. The original Transformer had eight in each layer; large modern models have dozens. Each head attends to the sequence in its own way, and the results of all of them are added into the columns.

When researchers went to look at what individual heads had learnt to do, they found a certain amount of intelligible specialisation. There are heads that attend from each word to the one before. There are heads that link verbs to their objects, and heads that link pronouns to the nouns they stand for. There are heads that seem to follow which bracket or quotation mark is currently open. And there are many heads whose behaviour no one has been able to put a name to.

Compute: the MLP

After the attention step, each column holds a blend of information that has been gathered from elsewhere. The second half of the layer processes it. This part is very plain. It is a small multi-layer perceptron, of the kind we met in chapter three: a matrix multiplication that expands the vector to something like four times its width, a non-linearity, and a second matrix multiplication that brings it back down.

The MLP is applied to each position separately. At this stage there is no communication between columns; that was the job of attention. Each column simply digests what it has received. And the result, once again, is added to the column's vector.

It would be easy to take these MLP blocks for an afterthought, but they hold about two-thirds of the weights in a typical Transformer. The best current evidence is that they are where much of the model's factual knowledge resides. An attention head might pull together the information that the sentence is about the Eiffel Tower, and that what is wanted is a location; it is then an MLP that responds to that combination by writing "Paris" into the column. One way to think about them is as an enormous associative memory: when this pattern shows up, add that.

The residual stream

You will have noticed that I keep saying "added". The output of attention is added to the column. The output of the MLP is added to the column. These are the residual connections from chapter four, and in the Transformer they are so central that people who study these models have a name for the running total. They call it the residual stream.

Here is the picture. Each column is a channel that runs straight up through the network from bottom to top. The token's embedding is put into it at the bottom. Then every attention head and every MLP, at every layer, reads from that channel and writes back into it by addition. No component ever overwrites the stream. Each one just adds its contribution to what is already there. At the top, whatever has accumulated in the stream is read off to make the prediction.

This is a different picture from the one we began with in chapter three, where each layer replaces its input with a brand new representation. Here, the layers are more like a succession of editors making notes on a shared document. An early layer may write "this token is a noun". A middle layer may read that, and write "and it is the subject of the verb 'fit'". A late layer may read both of those, and write "so the next word is probably an adjective describing size". All of the notes pile up in the same vector, which has room for them, because, as we saw in chapter five, a space of several thousand dimensions can hold a very great number of nearly independent directions.

The residual stream is also what makes the network trainable at depth, for the reason given in chapter four. Gradients flow straight down the stream from the output to every layer, without interruption. That is why Transformers can be stacked a hundred layers deep without the gradients vanishing. A normalisation step is also applied before each component, to keep the numbers in a sensible range.

Where am I? The problem of position

There is something odd about self-attention as I have described it. It has no sense of word order.

Look at the mechanism again. Each position's query is compared with each position's key, and the comparison depends only on the content of the vectors. Nothing in it knows that one token is the third in the sequence and another is the tenth. If you shuffled the words of the sentence, every token would receive exactly the same blend of information as before. To raw self-attention, a sentence is a bag of words. And that will not do; we began the last chapter with "dog bites man".

A recurrent network gets word order for nothing, since it reads the words in order. The Transformer gave that up when it gave up recurrence, and so position has to be put back in by hand. The method is to stamp each token with its position before it enters the network. Along with the embedding that says what the token is, you add a second vector that says where it is.

In the original paper, this was done in a way a physicist will enjoy. Each position was encoded as a set of sine and cosine waves of different frequencies, from very fast to very slow, sampled at that position. It is rather like the way the hands of a clock encode the time: a second hand, a minute hand and an hour hand, each sweeping round at its own rate, which together identify a moment uniquely. It works, because the relative offset between any two positions corresponds to a fixed rotation of each pair of sine and cosine components, and that is something a dot product can easily detect. Modern models use more refined schemes, the most popular of which rotates the query and key vectors by an angle that depends on their position. But the principle is unchanged: order is not built into the architecture, but supplied as additional information in the data.

That is a striking design choice, when you think about it. The Transformer is not, fundamentally, a model of sequences. It is a model of sets of vectors that can exchange information with one another, and the fact that they come in a sequence is just one more feature that those vectors may carry. That turns out to matter enormously, as we shall see in a moment.

No peeking

One further detail is needed to turn this into a language model, and it is the key to how efficiently these models can be trained.

We are training the network to predict the next token. So when it is working out its prediction for what follows position ten, it had better not be able to see position eleven. That would be cheating. The fix is easy: in the attention step, each position is allowed to attend only to itself and to earlier positions. Attention to later positions is blocked; their scores are set to negative infinity before the softmax, so that their weights are zero. This is known as causal masking. Information flows only from the past to the future.

And here is the benefit. Since each position sees only its own past, the column at position ten makes a legitimate prediction of token eleven, and the column at position eleven makes a legitimate prediction of token twelve, and so on, all in the same forward pass. Feed in a passage of a thousand tokens, and you get a thousand predictions back, each of which can be marked against the token that actually came next. A thousand training examples for the price of one, computed in parallel. Compare this with the recurrent network, which had to creep through the passage one step at a time.

When the model is generating text, on the other hand, it still has to go one token at a time, as there is nothing yet in the future to predict from: generate a token, append it, run the network again. That is why your chatbot types its answer out. But training is massively parallel, and training is where the expense lies.

To finish, the top of the stack. After the final layer, the vector in each column is multiplied by one last matrix, which gives a score for every token in the vocabulary. Those are the logits. Softmax turns them into probabilities, and the cross-entropy loss marks them against the truth. Backpropagation then carries the gradient down through every layer, into every head and every MLP, into the embedding table. And that completes the machine.

Why it mattered

So, why was this paper so important? There were other successful architectures in 2017. What was it about this one?

The first answer is parallelism. I said at the end of the last chapter that the recurrent network was a poor fit for the GPU. The Transformer is a perfect fit. Every position is processed at the same time. The whole computation is big matrix multiplications from top to bottom. That meant that for the first time, a language model could make full use of as much hardware as you could afford. The Transformer was not just a better model at a given size. It was a model that you could make a thousand times bigger and still be able to train.

The second answer is that it removed the problem of distance. Any token can draw on any other token directly, in one step, however far apart they are. Long-range dependency, the thing that recurrent networks fought with for twenty-five years, is simply not a difficulty.

The third answer is generality, and this goes back to that point about sets. The Transformer has very little inductive bias. It does not assume that its input is a sentence, or an image, or anything in particular. It assumes only that the input is a collection of vectors, and that they may need to share information. So you can use it for anything that can be chopped into pieces and turned into vectors. Chop an image into small square patches, treat every patch as a token, and you have a vision model that eventually came to outperform convolutional networks. Do the same with slices of audio, and you have speech recognition. With the amino acids in a protein, and you have the core of AlphaFold, which solved the problem of predicting protein structure and earned its makers a Nobel Prize. Before 2017, each of those fields had its own specialised architectures and its own community. Since then they have converged on one. One researcher, Andrej Karpathy, has described the Transformer as a general-purpose computer that happens to be differentiable, and so can be programmed by gradient descent in place of a programmer.

There is a cost, and you should know about it. Since every token attends to every other, the amount of computation grows as the square of the length of the sequence. Double the length of the text, and the attention step costs four times as much. This is the main reason that models have a limited context window, and a great deal of engineering effort has gone into pushing that limit from the few hundred tokens of 2017 to the million or more of today.

What is perhaps most remarkable of all is how little has changed since. If you were to open up one of the frontier models of today, you would find an architecture which the eight authors would recognise immediately. There have been adjustments: where the normalisation goes, how position is encoded, how the attention calculation is organised to save memory. But the design is the one from 2017: columns of vectors, layers of attention and MLPs, a residual stream. Nearly all the progress since then has come from somewhere else. It has come from what the model is trained on, and how, and above all from how big it is.

The people who grasped that first were not at Google. They were at a small research laboratory in San Francisco, where one of the founders was a man we have already met twice, and who believed that a large enough network, trained on enough data, would simply work. That laboratory was OpenAI, and the man was Ilya Sutskever.

The maths in brief

  • Stack the n position vectors into a matrix X (n × d). One attention head computes Q = X W_Q, K = X W_K, V = X W_V, then Attention = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √d_k + mask ) V. Q Kᵀ is the n × n table of all query-key dot products. The mask is −∞ above the diagonal (no looking ahead).
  • A layer (modern "pre-norm" form): x ← x + Attn(Norm(x)), then x ← x + MLP(Norm(x)).
  • MLP block: MLP(x) = W₂ · f(W₁ x), with the hidden width typically 4d.
  • Sinusoidal positions: PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d)), PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(…).
  • Output: logits = x_final · W_U; p = softmax(logits).
  • Cost: attention is O(n²·d) per layer; the MLPs are O(n·d²).
  • Sizes: the 2017 base model had 6 layers, d = 512, 8 heads, about 65 million weights. GPT-3 (2020) had 96 layers, d = 12,288, 96 heads, 175 billion weights.
