Chapter 8: The Generative Pre-trained Transformer

A neuron for sentiment

In 2017, the year of the Transformer paper, a young researcher at OpenAI named Alec Radford ran an experiment whose result surprised him. He took eighty-two million product reviews from Amazon, and trained a recurrent network on them to do one thing only: to predict the next character. Not the next word; the next letter or punctuation mark. It took a month.

Afterwards he examined the network to see what, if anything, it had learnt. Among its four thousand or so neurons he found one that was behaving in a remarkable way. Its value went up when the review was favourable, and down when it was unfavourable. Reading through a review a character at a time, that neuron kept a running assessment of the writer's opinion. When a review began with praise and then turned sour, with a "however", the neuron swung from positive to negative just at that point. And reading off that one neuron's value gave a better classifier of sentiment than systems that had been purpose-built and trained for the task.

Nobody had told the network anything about sentiment. No review had been labelled as positive or negative. The network had only been asked to guess the next character. But think about what helps with that. If you know that the reviewer is pleased, then you know that the next word is more likely to be "excellent" than "broken". An internal variable that keeps track of the reviewer's mood is useful for prediction, and so gradient descent had built one.

This was a small result, but to the people at OpenAI it pointed at something big. If predicting text forces a network to discover sentiment, without being asked to, what else might it be forced to discover? Grammar, certainly. Facts about the world, perhaps. Perhaps the rudiments of reasoning. And what if you ran the same experiment with a better architecture, on much more text?

The laboratory

OpenAI had been founded at the end of 2015 by a group that included Sam Altman, who at the time ran the start-up incubator Y Combinator; Elon Musk; and Ilya Sutskever, who was lured away from Google to be its chief scientist. It was set up as a non-profit, with a billion dollars in pledges and a mission statement about ensuring that artificial general intelligence would benefit all of humanity. Behind this lay a fear, shared by its founders, that this technology was going to be very powerful, and that it ought not to be left in the hands of Google alone.

For its first couple of years the lab was not notably focused on language. It worked on programs that learned to play video games, and on a robot hand. Radford's work on language models was a side project. But when the Transformer paper appeared, he and Sutskever saw straight away what it meant for the experiment they wanted to do. Here was an architecture that could be trained in parallel, at a scale that the recurrent network could never reach.

Pre-train, then fine-tune

In June 2018, OpenAI published the result. They took the Transformer, and kept just the half of it that generates text, with the causal masking I described in the last chapter. They trained it to predict the next token on the text of about seven thousand unpublished novels: romance, fantasy, adventure. The model had twelve layers and a hundred and seventeen million weights. They named it the generative pre-trained transformer, and it is now known as GPT-1.

The important word there is "pre-trained", and it describes a recipe with two steps. In the first step, pre-training, you train the model on a vast amount of unlabelled text, with the generic objective of predicting the next token. This is expensive, and you do it once. In the second step, fine-tuning, you take the pre-trained model and train it a little more, on a small set of labelled examples for the particular task that you care about: answering questions, say, or classifying legal documents. The weights start out from where pre-training left them, and need only to be nudged.

The reasoning is that most of what you need for any language task is common to all language tasks. You have to know what words mean, how sentences are built, and how the world that the sentences describe hangs together. Pre-training provides that general knowledge. Fine-tuning then has merely to teach the model the specific job, and it can do that from a few thousand examples, instead of the millions it would need if starting from scratch. The model is like a well-read graduate who has to be shown the particulars of a new job, and not like a newborn who has to be taught to read.

This was a familiar recipe in computer vision; it was standard practice after 2012 to take a network trained on ImageNet and to fine-tune it for, say, classifying skin lesions. Several groups made it work for language around the same time in 2018. GPT-1 was among them, and it set new records on a range of standard benchmarks. Models of this sort, big generic ones that are pre-trained once and then adapted to many uses, would come to be called foundation models.

The road not taken: BERT

GPT-1 was overshadowed almost at once. Four months later, in October 2018, a team at Google released a model that they called BERT. The name is an acronym, engineered so that the model could be named after a character from Sesame Street, in homage to an earlier model called ELMo.

BERT was a Transformer too, pre-trained on unlabelled text and then fine-tuned. But it used a different guessing game. GPT predicts the next word, having seen only the words to its left. BERT's designers reasoned that if what you want is to understand a sentence, you ought to look at all of it, in both directions. So in place of predicting the next word, BERT was trained to fill in blanks. Take a sentence, hide about fifteen per cent of the words at random, and ask the model to restore them, making use of the context on both sides.

On the benchmarks of the day, which tested comprehension: answering questions about a passage, deciding whether one sentence follows from another, BERT crushed everything else, GPT-1 included. Within a year, Google was using it to interpret the queries that people typed into its search engine. A cottage industry of variants grew up, and for a couple of years BERT, not GPT, was what most researchers meant by a language model.

But the design had a limitation, which turned out to matter more than anybody expected. A model that is trained to fill in blanks, with a view of both sides, cannot naturally write. It has no notion of "what comes next", since it was never asked to produce text from left to right. BERT could read, but it could not speak. GPT's left-to-right design looked, in 2018, like a handicap that it had accepted for no good reason. It proved to be the whole point, because a model that can generate text can do any task that can be expressed as text, and that covers just about everything.

Unicorns

OpenAI's response to BERT was not to alter its approach, but to scale it up. In February 2019 it announced GPT-2: essentially the same architecture, but with one and a half billion weights, more than ten times as many as before, trained on the text of some eight million web pages.

GPT-2 could write. Given an opening sentence, it would carry on for paragraphs of fluent, grammatical, mostly coherent prose. The most famous sample started from a prompt written by a human, to the effect that scientists had discovered a herd of unicorns in a remote valley in the Andes, and that the unicorns spoke perfect English. The model continued with a news story. It gave the lead scientist a name and a university. It quoted him. It speculated about where the unicorns had come from. It was all nonsense, naturally, but it was nonsense that maintained a theme and a cast of characters over several hundred words, which no machine had done before.

OpenAI made a decision at this point which was mocked at the time. It announced that it would not release the full model immediately, out of concern that it might be used to mass-produce fake news and spam. Many researchers considered this a publicity stunt, and the full model was released later that year without disaster. But it was the first time a laboratory had treated a language model as a thing that might be dangerous to publish, and it was not the last.

Something else about GPT-2 got less attention, but was more important. It could do things that it had not been fine-tuned to do. If you gave it a passage, followed by the letters TL, semicolon, DR, the internet's abbreviation for "too long, didn't read", it would write a summary. If you gave it a few pairs of English and French sentences and then an English sentence on its own, it would have a go at translating it. It did these things badly. But it did them without any further training, simply as a result of being asked in the right way.

Few-shot learners

That observation became the main theme of the next model. GPT-3 was described in a paper of May 2020. It had a hundred and seventy-five billion weights, over a hundred times as many as GPT-2, arranged in ninety-six layers. It was trained on several hundred billion tokens of text: a large crawl of the public web, a library of books, and the whole of the English-language Wikipedia, which made up about three per cent of the total. The training run was reckoned to have cost several million dollars in computing time. In the previous year, OpenAI had restructured itself to be able to raise that kind of money, creating a for-profit arm and accepting an investment of a billion dollars from Microsoft.

The paper's title was "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners", and this is what it meant. With earlier models, if you wanted the model to carry out a new task, you fine-tuned it; you changed its weights. With GPT-3, it was often enough to show it the task. You write out a few examples in the prompt. "Sea otter" becomes "loutre de mer". "Cheese" becomes "fromage". "Peppermint" becomes: and the model completes the pattern. Two or three examples would do, and sometimes just a plain description of what was wanted. The same frozen model, with not one weight altered, could translate, summarise, answer trivia questions, fix grammar, do simple arithmetic, and write passable computer code, according to what you put in front of it.

This ability is known as in-context learning, and it is a strange and important thing. Let me be exact about what is strange. We have said that learning, for a neural network, means changing the weights by gradient descent. Here, the weights are not changing at all. And yet the model is plainly, in some sense, learning a new task from examples, in the course of a single forward pass. Whatever adaptation there is, is going on in the activations: in those columns of vectors, as attention carries information from the examples to the place where the answer is wanted.

So a language model has two quite distinct kinds of memory, and the distinction comes up all the time. There are the weights: fixed after training, and holding all that the model took in from its training data. You could call that its long-term memory. And there is the context window: the text that is in front of it right now, which it can attend to directly. That is its working memory. What is in the weights is recalled imperfectly, in the way that you recall a book you read some years ago. What is in the context can be consulted exactly, like a document open on your desk. A great deal of the practical engineering of today consists of getting the right information into the context.

Nobody designed in-context learning. Nothing in the architecture or the loss function is specifically intended to yield it. It emerged, as the sentiment neuron did, because it helps with prediction. The internet is full of documents in which a pattern is set up and then continued: lists, tables, sets of worked examples, questions followed by answers. To predict those well, a model has to become good at spotting a pattern in the foregoing text and extending it. Do that with enough capacity, and on enough data, and what you end up with is a general-purpose pattern-continuing machine.

Why would prediction be enough?

It is worth stopping here to ask why this works, because the claim is, on the face of it, absurd: that a system trained only to guess the next word should come to translate languages and write programs.

The best statement of the argument that I know of is due to Ilya Sutskever, and it involves a detective novel. Suppose you are reading a mystery. There is a complicated plot, with many characters, clues, and red herrings. On the final page, the detective gathers everybody in the drawing room and says: "The murderer is", and then a name. Now, predict that next word. To do it well, to give a high probability to the correct name, you must have followed the plot. You must have kept track of who was where, and who was lying, and what the clues implied. The better you understood the story, the better your prediction will be.

The training data is full of things like this. To predict the next line of a mathematical proof, it helps to follow the mathematics. To predict the next move in the record of a chess game, it helps to know chess. To predict what a character in a story will say next, it helps to have a model of what that character believes and wants. The task of predicting the next token has no ceiling: there is always more to be gained by a deeper understanding of the process that produced the text. And the process that produced the text is human beings, thinking about the world. A network that is under relentless pressure to predict what people write is under pressure to model the people, and the world that they are writing about.

That is the optimistic view. There is a sceptical one too, and it deserves a hearing. Its most quoted expression is in a 2021 paper by the linguist Emily Bender and several co-authors, which described language models as stochastic parrots: systems that stitch together sequences of words according to statistical patterns, with no reference to meaning. On this view, the model does not understand the detective story. It has seen ten thousand detective stories, and has learnt what sort of name tends to come up at that point. It manipulates the form of language, and we, as readers, supply the meaning.

This argument has not been resolved, and to some extent it turns on what you mean by "understand". I will only say here that both sides can point to evidence. These models certainly do things that are hard to account for without some internal modelling of the situation described; we will see direct evidence of that in chapter twelve. They also fail in ways which no human being who understood the material would fail. And they have a characteristic flaw that follows directly from the way they are trained. The objective is to produce text that is probable; it is not to produce text that is true. Where the model knows the answer, the probable continuation and the true one coincide. Where it does not, it will still produce a fluent and confident continuation, because fluent and confident is how text of that sort usually reads. It will make up a citation, or a biography, or a legal precedent, without any sign of hesitation. The polite term for this is hallucination, and it remains the best-known weakness of the technology.

The document completer

There is a point about these models at this stage in the story which is easily forgotten now, and which clears up a good deal. GPT-3, as it was released, was not an assistant. It did not answer questions. It continued documents.

If you typed in a question, it might answer it. Or it might add three further questions, since what it was looking at appeared to be a list of questions. Or it might carry on in the voice of a forum post, complete with a rude reply from another user. A model like this, taken straight from pre-training, is called a base model, and the right way to think about it is as a simulator of documents. It has read a large part of the internet, and when it is given the beginning of some text, it asks itself, in effect: what sort of document is this, who would be writing it, and what would they write next?

So to get useful work out of GPT-3 you had to be artful. To get a good answer to a medical question, you did not just ask the question. You wrote the opening of a document in which a good answer would be likely to come next: perhaps the header of an article from a medical journal, or the transcript of an interview with an eminent physician. The model would then do its best to continue in character. The craft of contriving such openings was called prompt engineering, and for a while it looked like a new profession.

The step from that slightly wild document-completing engine to the helpful assistant that you know today is the subject of chapter ten. First, though, we should look squarely at what OpenAI had done between 2018 and 2020. The architecture of GPT-3 was that of GPT-1. The training objective was the same. The one thing that changed was size: a thousand times more weights, and a thousand times more data. And as the model grew, it did not simply become a bit more accurate. It acquired abilities that the smaller models did not have at all.

Why did they believe that this would work? And how far could it go? The answer was a set of empirical regularities, as smooth and as surprising as anything in physics. They are called the scaling laws.

Key terms

  • Pre-training: the expensive, generic phase. Next-token prediction on a huge unlabelled corpus.
  • Fine-tuning: further training of a pre-trained model on a smaller, specific dataset.
  • Base model: a model straight out of pre-training; a document completer.
  • Zero-shot / few-shot: doing a task from a description alone / from a few examples in the prompt, with no weight updates.
  • In-context learning: adaptation that happens in the activations during a forward pass, not in the weights.
  • Decoder-only vs encoder-only: GPT-style (causal mask, predicts next token, can generate) vs BERT-style (sees both directions, fills in blanks, cannot generate).

The GPT series at a glance

  • GPT-1 (June 2018): 12 layers, 117 million weights, ~1 billion words of books.
  • GPT-2 (February 2019): 48 layers, 1.5 billion weights, 40 GB of web text.
  • GPT-3 (May 2020): 96 layers, 175 billion weights, ~300 billion tokens.
