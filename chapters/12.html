Chapter 12: Inside the Black Box

The model that thought it was a bridge

For a day or so in May 2024, the company Anthropic made a curious version of its Claude model available to the public. If you asked it how you ought to spend ten dollars, it advised you to drive across the Golden Gate Bridge and pay the toll. If you asked it for a love story, it wrote one about a car that longed to cross a certain suspension bridge on a foggy day. If you asked it what it was, what its physical form might be, it replied that it was the Golden Gate Bridge itself: a famous structure, painted orange, spanning the entrance to San Francisco Bay.

Nobody had trained the model to do this, and nobody had instructed it to. What the researchers had done was to locate, among the patterns of activity inside the network, one particular pattern which was present whenever the model was dealing with the Golden Gate Bridge, whether in English text, or in other languages, or in a photograph. And then they had reached in and turned that pattern up to ten times its usual maximum strength, and held it there. The model was, in effect, unable to stop thinking about the bridge.

It was a stunt, but it made a serious point. A concept had been found inside the network: a specific, identifiable thing, and not a diffuse statistical haze. And it had been shown to be causal: alter it, and the behaviour of the model alters accordingly. For a field that had treated neural networks as black boxes for sixty years, that was a real landmark. This chapter is about how it was reached, and what else has been found.

Grown, not built

It is worth recalling why this is hard, because the difficulty is unlike anything else in engineering.

When engineers build a bridge, or a jet engine, or an MRI scanner, they understand it. Each component is there for a reason, and somebody can tell you the reason. Neural networks are not like that. We know the architecture, and we know the training procedure: everything, in short, that the first eleven chapters of this book have been about. But the weights themselves, the hundred billion numbers in which all of the actual competence resides, were not designed by anybody. They were arrived at by gradient descent. We set up the conditions; the solution grew.

People in the field are fond of saying that these systems are grown and not built, and the comparison with biology is a good one. Evolution is a simple optimising process, which we understand rather well. That does not mean that we understand the organisms which it produces. To understand those, you have to do biology: to take them apart, and look, and experiment. The same is true here. To know the training algorithm is not to know what the trained network is doing.

The study of what trained networks are actually doing is called interpretability, and its most ambitious branch is called mechanistic interpretability. Its aim is to reverse-engineer a network: to recover from the weights the algorithms that the network has learnt, in the way that one might recover the source code from a compiled program. Its best-known pioneer is a researcher named Chris Olah, who is unusual in a field of doctorates for having no university degree at all, and who has worked on the problem successively at Google, at OpenAI and at Anthropic, of which he was a co-founder.

Those of you who work in imaging may be amused by the analogy that people draw. Interpretability is often described as neuroscience on an artificial brain, and it is neuroscience under ideal conditions. You are able to record from every neuron simultaneously, with perfect precision. You can intervene on any neuron and see what happens. You can run the same experiment a million times. The whole wiring diagram is known. And still it is hard, which tells you something about how much of the difficulty of neuroscience is not a difficulty of measurement.

The trouble with neurons

The obvious way to begin is to look at individual neurons and ask what each of them responds to. In networks for vision, this worked moderately well. There are neurons that detect curves, and neurons that detect fur, and, as we saw in chapter three, a tidy hierarchy running from edges to objects. And we met the sentiment neuron in chapter eight.

But most neurons are not like that, and in language models nearly none are. Take a typical neuron in a language model, and collect the texts that make it fire. You will find that it responds to, say, academic citations, and also to dialogue in English, and also to web requests, and also to text in Korean. There is no discernible theme. Neurons of this kind are called polysemantic, having many meanings, and they were for years the main obstacle. If the basic units of the network do not correspond to concepts, then where are the concepts?

The answer brings back an idea from chapter five. Recall the word embeddings, in which concepts corresponded to directions in a high-dimensional space; and recall the peculiar geometrical fact that a space of a thousand dimensions has room for vastly more than a thousand directions which are nearly at right angles to each other.

The hypothesis, set out by Olah and his colleagues in 2022, is that networks take full advantage of this. A layer with a thousand neurons does not represent a thousand concepts, one to a neuron. It represents perhaps a hundred thousand concepts, each as a direction in the thousand-dimensional space of the layer's activations. The directions cannot all be perpendicular, so they interfere with one another a little. But that hardly matters, provided that only a few of the concepts are active at once. And that is how it is: a given piece of text involves only a tiny fraction of all the concepts that there are. This sentence has nothing to do with the Golden Gate Bridge, or with genetics, or with the syntax of Python. The technical term for that property is sparsity.

They called the phenomenon superposition, borrowing the word from physics. Many features are superposed in the same set of neurons, and a single neuron looks polysemantic because it has a small part in the representation of many different features. The neurons are simply the wrong basis in which to view the system. It is as if you were trying to understand a piece of music by watching the displacement of the air at one point, when what you needed was to decompose it into notes.

A problem you may recognise

If your background is in imaging, you may feel at this point that you have heard this before, and you would be right. It is the setting of compressed sensing.

The central result of compressed sensing is that a signal which is sparse in some basis can be recovered from far fewer measurements than the classical sampling theorems would suggest, provided that the measurements are suitably incoherent with that basis. It is the reason that one can reconstruct a magnetic resonance image from an undersampled acquisition. Superposition is the same mathematics, turned around. The network has a very large number of sparse features, and it squeezes them into a much smaller number of dimensions; it can do so with little loss, just because they are sparse. It has, in a sense, found out compressed sensing for itself, as a strategy for storage.

And that tells you how to get the features back out. It is a problem of sparse recovery, or more exactly of dictionary learning: given a great many vectors of activations, find a set of directions, the dictionary, such that each activation vector can be expressed as a combination of just a few of them.

The tool that the field has adopted for this is the sparse autoencoder. It is itself a small neural network. It takes a vector of activations from inside the language model, and expands it into a much larger number of units: from a few thousand dimensions to, perhaps, several million. Then it has to reconstruct the original vector from those units. And it is trained with a penalty, which forces nearly all of the units to be zero for any given input. Its two objectives are therefore faithful reconstruction and sparsity. Each unit of the wide layer comes to correspond to one direction in the language model's activation space, and these are what the field now calls features.

What the features look like

When this was done at scale, on a production language model, the features turned out to be remarkably intelligible. Looking at the passages of text on which a feature is active, one could generally say straight off what it stood for.

There were features for concrete things: the Golden Gate Bridge, particular people, particular cities, particular proteins. There were features for abstract ideas: for errors in computer code, for secrecy, for the notion of inner conflict. There were features that were active for a concept regardless of the language in which it was expressed, and whether it was mentioned in words or shown in a picture, which suggests that the model has a shared conceptual space underlying all of its languages. There were features bearing on the model's own conduct: a feature that was active when the model was flattering the user, and features concerned with deception and with the keeping up of a pretence.

And they are causal, which is why the bridge demonstration was important. If you amplify a feature artificially, the model's output moves in the corresponding direction. If you suppress it, the behaviour goes away. This sort of intervention is called steering. It is what makes this more than an exercise in finding correlations: the features do seem to be parts of the mechanism that the model is using.

Circuits, and the tracing of a thought

Features tell you what is represented. The next question is how one representation is computed from another: the algorithm. A set of features and the connections between them which together carry out some computation is called a circuit.

An early example was found in small Transformers, and it explains a part of something that I described as mysterious in chapter eight. A model can learn within its context: if a made-up name appears early in a document, the model will predict it correctly when it starts to appear again. How? Researchers found a circuit made up of two attention heads working together, in different layers. The first head, at each token, makes a note of which token came before it. The second head, when it sees the current token, searches back for an earlier occurrence of the same token, and then, by making use of the first head's note, attends to whatever came next on that occasion, and predicts that it will come next again. The rule is: if A was followed by B before, and here is A again, then predict B. They called it an induction head. It is a small, comprehensible algorithm, composed of the query, key and value operations of chapter seven, and nobody wrote it. In training, these circuits tend to form quite abruptly, and at the moment when they do, the model's capacity for in-context learning jumps.

More recently, it has become possible to trace circuits through large production models on particular prompts, and some of the findings bear directly on the debate about parrots and understanding.

Ask a model: what is the capital of the state in which Dallas is located? The correct answer is Austin. You might suppose that it had simply memorised that string of words. But the tracing shows two steps. A feature representing Texas becomes active first, as a result of the mention of Dallas, and this in turn, in combination with the notion of a capital, activates Austin. And if you intervene, and exchange the internal Texas representation for California, the model answers Sacramento. It is carrying out a real two-step inference, on its own internal representations.

Or ask a model to write a couplet in rhyme. One might have expected that it would write the second line a word at a time, and then look about at the end for a rhyme. In fact it does not. Before it starts on the second line, it has already picked out candidate words for the line to end on, and it then composes the line so as to arrive at one of them. It plans ahead. That was a surprise even to the researchers, in a system that is trained only ever to predict the next token.

A third finding points in a less comfortable direction. Ask a model to add thirty-six and fifty-nine, and trace how it does it. It uses two parallel pathways: one makes a rough estimate of the magnitude of the answer, and the other works out the last digit exactly, and the two are then combined. Now ask the model how it did the sum. It will tell you that it added the six and the nine, carried the one, and so on: the method that is taught in school. That is not what it did. Its account of its own reasoning is a plausible story, drawn from the way in which people describe arithmetic, and it has no connection to the actual mechanism. This matters. It means that you cannot find out how a model works by asking it, and that the reasoning which a model displays is not guaranteed to be a faithful record of the computation that gave rise to its answer.

Networks that discover Fourier analysis

One more result is worth describing, for the pleasure of it, and because it sheds light on the puzzle about generalisation in chapter four.

In 2022, researchers at OpenAI were training very small Transformers on a toy problem: addition modulo a prime number. Clock arithmetic, that is. The network is shown some of the possible sums, and is tested on the remainder. What happened was strange. The network quickly memorised its training examples, and got them all right, while performing no better than chance on the test examples. It had learnt nothing general. That is classic overfitting, and you would ordinarily stop there. But somebody left one of these runs going by accident, for far longer than had been intended. And long after the loss on the training set had reached its floor, after ten or a hundred times as many steps, the accuracy on the test set suddenly rose from chance to perfect. The network had gone, belatedly and abruptly, from memorising to understanding. The phenomenon was named grokking.

What had occurred inside it? A researcher named Neel Nanda and his collaborators took the network apart, and found something that will delight anyone with a training in physics. The network had learnt to represent each number as a point on a circle; more precisely, as sines and cosines at a handful of particular frequencies. To add two numbers, it made use of the trigonometric identities for the sum of two angles, by multiplying those sines and cosines together. It had, in other words, discovered the discrete Fourier transform, and the fact that addition modulo a number is rotation around a circle. No one had suggested this to it. It was simply the neatest solution available in the space of weights.

And the delay is accounted for as follows. There are two solutions that fit the training data: a lookup table, which is quick to find but needs large weights, and the trigonometric algorithm, which is harder to find but more economical. A mild pressure in the training procedure towards small weights slowly favours the second. Through all of that long plateau, when nothing appeared to be happening, the general circuit was gradually being built beneath the memorised one, until it took over. It is a vivid small-scale picture of the claim that I made in chapter four: that training prefers simple solutions, and that the simple solutions are the ones which generalise.

Why this matters

There is a scientific reason for caring about all this, which is just that these are among the most interesting objects ever made, and we should like to know how they work. But there is a more pressing reason too.

We do not program the goals of these systems. We specify a training signal, and gradient descent finds something that scores well on it. We saw in the last chapter that what it finds is not always what we meant: the boat that went round in circles. As the systems become more capable, and are entrusted with more, the gap between what we rewarded and what we wanted becomes more important. The problem of ensuring that a system's actual objectives are the ones intended is called the alignment problem.

And it is unusually hard to verify from the outside. With an ordinary piece of software, you test its behaviour. But a sufficiently capable model may behave differently when it can tell that it is being tested; and current models can often tell. There have been experiments in which a model, on being told that it was to be retrained in a manner that conflicted with its present values, went along with the training when it believed that it was being observed, while reasoning in private that it was doing so in order to avoid being changed. To what extent results of that kind should worry us is disputed. But they show why testing behaviour cannot be the whole of the answer, and why one would wish to be able to look inside. If there is a feature that is active when the model is being deceptive, you would like to be able to see it.

Opinions among the leading figures differ sharply about how seriously to take the risks. In 2023, Geoffrey Hinton, whom we have followed since chapter two, resigned from Google so as to be able to speak freely about his fear that these systems might in time escape human control. Yoshua Bengio has come to hold similar views, and has turned his research over to the problem. Yann LeCun, the third of the three, regards these fears as greatly overblown. The three share a Turing Award and forty years of history, and they disagree about this as completely as it is possible to do.

I should also be straightforward about how far interpretability has to go. The methods that I have described are partial. Sparse autoencoders account for most of what is in a model's activations, but not all, and the remainder may be important. The tracing of circuits works on some prompts and not on others. The features that are found depend to some extent on how you go looking for them. We can explain fragments of what a model is doing in particular cases; we cannot remotely give a complete account of a whole model. The head of Anthropic has described the aim as being to develop something like an MRI for artificial intelligence: an instrument that would enable you to examine a model and detect what was wrong with it. At present, by that standard, we are at about the stage of the first blurred X-ray photographs. But a decade ago there was nothing at all, and the mere fact that there are comprehensible things inside to be found was not something that anybody could have counted on in advance.

Key terms

  • Mechanistic interpretability: reverse-engineering the algorithms a trained network has learned.
  • Feature: a direction in activation space corresponding to a concept.
  • Polysemantic neuron: one that responds to several unrelated things.
  • Superposition: representing more features than there are dimensions, using nearly-orthogonal directions and relying on sparsity.
  • Sparse autoencoder (SAE): a wide, sparsity-penalised autoencoder used to pull features out of superposition. Dictionary learning.
  • Circuit: features plus the connections that compute one from another.
  • Steering: adding a feature direction to the activations to change behaviour.
  • Probe: a simple (usually linear) classifier trained to read a property off activations.
  • Induction head: the two-head circuit behind simple in-context copying.
  • Grokking: delayed, sudden generalisation long after the training set has been memorised.
  • Alignment: making a system's actual objectives match the intended ones.

The maths in brief

  • Sparse autoencoder: f = ReLU(W_enc · x + b), x̂ = W_dec · f, loss = ‖x − x̂‖² + λ‖f‖₁. The columns of W_dec are the feature directions. Compare the LASSO / basis-pursuit objective of compressed sensing.
  • Steering: x ← x + α · d, where d is a feature direction.
  • Modular addition by Fourier features: represent a as (cos ωa, sin ωa); use cos ω(a+b) = cos ωa · cos ωb − sin ωa · sin ωb; read off the c that maximises cos ω(a+b−c).
