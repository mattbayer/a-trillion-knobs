Chapter 5: Words into Vectors

The guessing game

In about 1950, Claude Shannon played a game with his wife, Betty. He would pick a passage from a book, and she would try to guess it, one letter at a time. For each letter she guessed until she got it right, and he wrote down how many guesses it had taken. Then on to the next letter.

Shannon was, at that point, two years on from publishing the paper that founded information theory, and he was using the game to measure something: the amount of information in written English. What he found was that Betty needed very few guesses. Most of the time she got the letter on her first try. Given the text so far, the next letter of English is extremely predictable. Shannon calculated that each letter carries, on average, only about one bit of real information, when in principle a choice among twenty-six letters and a space could carry nearly five. English is about three-quarters redundant.

Think about what Betty Shannon was using to play that game. She was using her knowledge of spelling, obviously. And of grammar. But she was also using her knowledge of what the passage was about, and of what kinds of things people say, and of how the world works. To predict that the next word after "the surgeon picked up the" is more likely to be "scalpel" than "trombone", you have to know something about surgery. Predicting text well requires understanding it, and the better your understanding, the better your predictions.

That observation is the foundation of everything in part two of this book. The entire technology of large language models is the playing of Shannon's guessing game by a neural network: given the text so far, predict what comes next. It sounds like a party trick. It turned out to be a way of pouring human knowledge into a machine.

What is a language model?

Let me define terms. A language model is a system that, given a sequence of words, assigns a probability to every possible next word. After "the cat sat on the", it might put thirty per cent on "mat", ten per cent on "floor", eight per cent on "sofa", and a tiny fraction of a per cent on "parliament".

There is a simple fact from probability theory that makes this more powerful than it sounds. The probability of a whole sentence is the probability of its first word, times the probability of the second word given the first, times the probability of the third given the first two, and so on. So a model that can predict the next word implicitly defines a probability for every possible text of any length. Nothing has been lost by reducing the problem to one word at a time.

And once you have such a model, you can make it write. Give it a starting text. Ask for the probabilities of the next word. Pick a word according to those probabilities, as though rolling a weighted die. Add it to the end of the text. Then ask again. Each new word is chosen in light of everything written so far, including the words the model has just produced itself. This loop has a name, autoregressive generation, and it is exactly what is happening when you watch a chatbot's answer appear on your screen a word at a time. That is not a visual effect. The model really is producing one piece at a time, and each piece is fed back in to produce the next.

Counting, and its limits

Shannon himself built the first language models, and he did it by counting. Take a large body of text, and for every pair of words, count how often the second follows the first. Now, to generate text, look at the last word you wrote, look up everything that ever followed it, and choose one in proportion to how often it did. This is called a bigram model. If you look back two words rather than one, it is a trigram model, and in general these are called n-gram models.

Such models produce text that is locally plausible and globally demented. Each phrase of three or four words is one that a person might write; but the sentence wanders off without ever arriving anywhere, because the model has no memory of anything further back than a few words. On the companion site there is a demonstration that lets you build these models from a sample of text and watch the output become more coherent as you lengthen the memory.

So why not just lengthen it? Look back twenty words instead of two. The problem is the one that statisticians call the curse of dimensionality. The number of possible sequences of twenty words is unimaginably vast, and almost none of them has ever been written. If your model works by looking up what followed this exact sequence in the past, then for almost any twenty-word sequence the answer is that there is no past: it has never occurred before. Counting can only tell you about what you have seen.

And notice what a counting model does not know. Suppose it has seen the sentence "the cat is sleeping on the sofa" many times. It has learned nothing whatever from that about "the dog is sleeping on the sofa" or "a kitten was napping on the couch". To the counting model, "cat" and "dog" are simply two different symbols, as unrelated as "cat" and "carburettor". It has no notion that words can be similar to one another. For it to generalise, it would need some representation of words in which similar words are close together.

N-gram models were nevertheless the workhorse of speech recognition and machine translation for some forty years, and I should not be too rude about them. But to get past them, the field needed an answer to a question that sounds easy and is not. How do you feed a word to a neural network?

Tokens

A network takes in numbers. Text is made of symbols. So the first step, always, is to chop the text into pieces, and assign each piece a number.

The pieces are called tokens. You might guess that a token is a word, and in early systems it was. But words are awkward. There are too many of them; new ones are invented daily; and what should the system do with a misspelling, or a name, or a string of computer code? You could instead use individual characters, which solves all those problems, but then even a short paragraph is a very long sequence, and the network must spend much of its effort simply learning to spell.

Modern systems split the difference. Their tokens are fragments, somewhere between a letter and a word, which are chosen automatically by an algorithm that scans a large body of text and merges the most frequent combinations of characters, again and again, until it has built up a vocabulary of the desired size. Typically that is somewhere from fifty thousand to a couple of hundred thousand tokens. Common words get a token to themselves. Rare words are assembled from pieces; "tokenisation" might come out as "token", then "isation". Anything at all can be represented, in the worst case by spelling it out letter by letter.

As a rule of thumb for English, one token is about three-quarters of a word. And tokens are the unit in which nearly everything about language models is measured. The amount of text a model can take into account at once, called its context window, is counted in tokens. So is the size of the training data, and so is the price that the companies charge. When someone says that a model was trained on ten trillion tokens, or that it has a context window of a million tokens, you can now translate.

From symbols to geometry

So now each token is a number, an index into the vocabulary. Token number 3,797, say. But that number is just a label. Token 3,797 is not in any meaningful sense close to token 3,798. We are back to the problem of "cat" and "carburettor".

The solution is one of the most consequential ideas in the whole field, and it is an idea a physicist will find natural. Represent each token not as a label, but as a point in a space. Give every token in the vocabulary its own vector: a list of a few hundred, or a few thousand, numbers. These vectors are called embeddings, because each word is embedded as a point in a high-dimensional space.

Where do the numbers in these vectors come from? This is the elegant part. They are weights. They begin as random numbers, and they are learned, by backpropagation, in just the same way as every other weight in the network. The complete set of embeddings is simply a big table, with one row for each token in the vocabulary, and the first thing the network does with a token is to look up its row. During training, the gradient flows all the way back to that table and nudges each word's vector in whichever direction makes the predictions better.

Think about what that pressure does. If "cat" and "dog" tend to be followed by similar words, and they do: "is sleeping", "wants to go out", "needs to go to the vet", then the network makes better predictions if it treats them alike, and the cheapest way to treat them alike is to give them similar vectors. So, over the course of training, words that are used in similar ways drift together in the space. Nobody tells the network that cats and dogs are both animals. It moves them close because that helps with the guessing game.

This idea was set out in a 2003 paper by Yoshua Bengio and his colleagues in Montreal. Linguists had, in a sense, anticipated it half a century before. It is called the distributional hypothesis, and its best-known statement is from the English linguist John Firth in 1957: you shall know a word by the company it keeps.

King minus man plus woman

The idea became famous a decade later. In 2013, a team at Google led by a Czech researcher named Tomas Mikolov published a method called word2vec. It was a deliberately simple and stripped-down neural network, hardly deep at all. Its one task was to take a word and predict the words that appear near it. Its simplicity meant that it was fast, fast enough to be trained on billions of words of text, and when the training was done, you threw away the network and kept the embeddings.

Those embeddings had a property that astonished people. Similar words were close together, as expected. But beyond that, the directions in the space carried meaning. Take the vector for "king". Subtract from it the vector for "man". Add the vector for "woman". Then ask which word's vector is closest to the point where you have arrived. The answer is "queen".

It works for other relationships too. Paris minus France plus Italy lands near Rome. "Walking" minus "walk" plus "swim" lands near "swimming". What this means is that there is a consistent direction in the space that corresponds to the difference between male and female, and another for the relation of a capital to its country, and another for a verb's tense. Concepts have become directions. Relationships between meanings have become geometric relationships between vectors, and you can do arithmetic on them.

Nobody designed the space to have this structure. It fell out of gradient descent, applied to nothing more than the task of predicting which words turn up near which. The examples were somewhat cherry-picked, I should say, and the arithmetic does not always work so neatly. But the central finding has held up and has only grown in importance: neural networks represent concepts as directions in a high-dimensional space.

It is worth pausing over the high-dimensional part, since it is where our intuitions are least reliable. In three dimensions, you can have just three directions that are all at right angles to one another. In a thousand dimensions you can have a thousand. But there is a much stranger fact about high-dimensional spaces. If you are willing to accept directions that are only nearly perpendicular, at eighty-five degrees to each other, say, rather than ninety, then the number of them that you can fit in is not a thousand, but grows exponentially with the dimension. A space of a few thousand dimensions has room for many millions of almost independent directions. So an embedding vector is not a list of a thousand features. It can be a compressed encoding of vastly more. This fact will turn out to be essential when, in chapter twelve, we look at attempts to read what is going on inside these networks.

The free lunch of self-supervision

There is something about word2vec that is easy to pass over, and it may be the most important thing in this chapter. Where did the training labels come from?

Recall ImageNet. It worked because of an enormous effort by tens of thousands of human beings to label millions of pictures by hand. That kind of learning, from examples that people have labelled with the right answers, is called supervised learning, and its bottleneck is plain: human labour.

Now look at the guessing game. The task is to predict the next word. The right answer is the next word. It is sitting right there in the text. No one has to label anything. Every sentence that anyone has ever written is a supply of training examples, complete with answers, free of charge. This is called self-supervised learning: the data supervises itself.

The significance of this is hard to overstate. It means that the supply of training data for language is, in effect, the whole of the internet, and every digitised book, and every scientific paper, and all the publicly posted computer code. That is trillions of words. And the training objective that unlocks all of it is the one Shannon was playing with in 1950. In the last chapter I said that the choice of what question to ask the network might be the most powerful lever of all. This is what I meant. The question "what comes next?" needs no labels, and has no ceiling, in the sense that you can always get better at it by understanding more.

Turning scores into probabilities

There are two more pieces of machinery to put in place, both at the output end of the network. Each of them will be familiar to a physicist, wearing an unfamiliar name.

A language model has to output a probability for every token in its vocabulary. But what the final layer of a network naturally produces is just a list of numbers, one per token, which can be anything: positive or negative, large or small. Those raw scores are called logits. To turn them into probabilities, you need to make them all positive, and make them sum to one. The standard way to do that is to take the exponential of each score, and then divide by the sum of all the exponentials. This operation is called the softmax.

If you have studied statistical mechanics, you have seen that formula before. It is the Boltzmann distribution. The logits play the part of negative energies, and the sum in the denominator is the partition function. And the parallel goes further. In physics, there is a temperature in the exponent, which controls how strongly the system prefers its low-energy states. Language models have just the same thing, and it is even called temperature. Before applying the softmax, you divide all of the logits by a temperature. At a low temperature the distribution sharpens, and the model nearly always chooses its single most probable token; its output becomes predictable and repetitive. At a high temperature the distribution flattens, less likely tokens get their chance, and the output becomes more surprising, more creative and, eventually, incoherent. When a developer adjusts the temperature setting on a language model, that is literally what is being adjusted.

The second piece is the loss function. The model has produced a probability distribution over the next token. We know which token actually came next. How wrong was the model? The standard answer is to look at the probability that the model gave to the correct token, and take the negative of its logarithm. If the model gave the right answer a probability of one, the loss is zero. If it gave the right answer a probability of one in a thousand, the loss is large. This is the cross-entropy loss, and it is the quantity that nearly every large language model is trained to minimise.

It has a nice interpretation, which brings us back to Shannon. The negative logarithm of a probability is a measure of surprise: how many bits of information you gain on learning that the thing happened. So the cross-entropy loss is the model's average surprise at the actual text. Training a language model means making it, on average, as unsurprised as possible by what human beings write. It is also, exactly, the number of bits per token that you would need to compress the text using the model's predictions. Prediction and compression are the same thing. A good language model is a good compressor of human writing, and the strongest argument that it must have learnt something about the world is that you cannot compress what you do not, in some sense, understand.

What a single vector cannot do

So by 2013 the field had a way to turn words into vectors that capture meaning, and an inexhaustible source of training signal. What it did not yet have was a good way to deal with words in combination.

The trouble with word2vec is that it gives one vector to each word, and words do not work like that. The word "bank" in "river bank" and in "bank account" would get the same vector, a muddy average of two unrelated meanings. The word "it" means nothing at all until you know what it refers to. Meaning depends on context: on the other words in the sentence, on their order, and on words that may be a long way off. "The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big." What was too big? You know at once that it was the trophy. Change "big" to "small", and "it" becomes the suitcase. To work that out, you have to combine information from across the whole sentence, along with some knowledge about how objects fit inside other objects.

What was needed was a network that reads a sequence of word vectors and produces representations of the words in context. For a quarter of a century, the leading candidate for that job was a design called the recurrent neural network. Its struggles, and the workaround that grew into its replacement, are the subject of the next chapter.

The maths in brief

  • Chain rule of probability: P(w₁…wₙ) = Π P(wₜ | w₁…wₜ₋₁).
  • Embedding: a matrix E of shape (vocabulary size × d). Token i is represented by row E[i].
  • Softmax with temperature: pᵢ = exp(zᵢ/T) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ/T). Compare Boltzmann: pᵢ = exp(−Eᵢ/kT) / Z.
  • Cross-entropy loss for one prediction: ℓ = −log p(correct token). Averaged over text, in base 2, this is bits per token. Perplexity is exp(mean loss): the effective number of equally likely choices the model is hesitating between.
  • Near-orthogonality: in d dimensions, two random unit vectors have a dot product of typical size 1/√d.
