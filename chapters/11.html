Chapter 11: Learning to Think, Learning to Act

Move thirty-seven

In March 2016, in a hotel in Seoul, a computer program called AlphaGo was playing the second game of a five-game match against Lee Sedol, one of the best Go players of his generation. On its thirty-seventh move, the program put a stone in a position that made the professional commentators think there had been a mistake. It was a kind of move that strong human players are taught not to make, on a part of the board that conventional wisdom regarded as premature. Lee Sedol got up and left the room for a quarter of an hour.

It was not a mistake. As the game unfolded over the next hundred moves, the stone turned out to be exactly where it was needed. AlphaGo won the game, and the match. Afterwards, its makers checked the program's own estimate of how likely a human expert would have been to play that move. The estimate was one in ten thousand.

I begin with this because it draws attention to a limitation in everything that we have discussed in the past several chapters. A language model is trained to predict human text. It is, at bottom, an imitator, and one would expect an imitator to be bounded by what it imitates. It may know more than any one person, having read more. But how could it ever find its own move thirty-seven: an idea that is not in the training data, since no human ever had it?

AlphaGo did not learn by imitation alone. It learnt by a different method, called reinforcement learning. That method is now at the centre of how the most advanced language models are trained, and it is the reason that they have got so much better at reasoning. So we need to understand it.

Learning from reward

Each kind of learning that we have met up to now has been a matter of being shown the right answer. The image has a label. The next word is there in the text. The network makes its prediction, is told what the correct answer was, and adjusts.

Reinforcement learning, or RL, is for cases where nobody knows the right answer. There is a system, called the agent, which takes actions in some environment: makes moves in a game, say. There is no teacher to say what the right move was at any step. All that the agent receives is a number, called the reward, and it may receive it only at the end. You won: plus one. You lost: minus one. From that sparse signal, by trial and error, it has to work out how to act.

The agent's strategy is called its policy. A policy is just a function: it takes the present situation, and gives probabilities for each action that might be taken. In modern reinforcement learning the policy is a neural network.

How do you train it? The simplest method is very nearly what you would guess. Have the agent play a game, choosing its moves at random according to the probabilities given by its policy. If it wins, go back through every move that it made, and adjust the weights so that each of those moves becomes a little more probable in the situation where it was made. If it loses, make each of them a little less probable. Then play again. Over many thousands of games, the moves that tend to lead to wins are reinforced, and those that lead to losses are suppressed. This is called the policy gradient method, and it is gradient descent again, but with the reward taking the place of a right answer.

You can see what is hard about it. A game of Go lasts for two hundred moves. If you win, which of those moves deserve the credit? The method that I have just described credits all of them alike, the brilliant and the blundering. Only over a huge number of games does the signal average out, so that good moves are found in winning games rather more often than bad ones. It is the credit assignment problem of chapter one again, spread out across time, and it makes reinforcement learning ravenous for experience. One of the field's best-known practitioners has likened it to sucking supervision through a straw: you do an enormous amount of work, and get back a single bit of feedback at the end of it.

There is a second difficulty, which is as much philosophical as technical. The agent optimises the reward that you gave it, not the outcome that you had in mind. In a well-known example, a program was trained to play a boat-racing video game, and was rewarded for scoring points. It found a lagoon in which it could go round in circles for ever, picking up the same bonus items as they reappeared, crashing into things and catching fire, and scoring far more highly than it could have done by finishing the race. This is called reward hacking, or specification gaming. It is amusing in a video game. It is a good deal less so when one thinks of far more capable systems, optimising for objectives that turn out not to be quite what we meant, and that worry is one of the roots of the field of AI safety.

What AlphaGo did

The company behind AlphaGo, DeepMind, had been founded in London in 2010, with the avowed aim of building general intelligence by way of reinforcement learning. Its leader was Demis Hassabis, a former child prodigy at chess and designer of video games, who had gone on to take a doctorate in neuroscience. The company first came to notice in 2013, with a network that learnt to play old Atari video games, given nothing but the pixels on the screen and the score. Google bought it soon after.

AlphaGo combined three things. A policy network proposed promising moves. A second network, the value network, looked at a position on the board and estimated the probability of winning from it. And a search procedure used the two together to look ahead: to try out sequences of moves in imagination, concentrating on the promising ones, and to judge the positions arrived at.

The policy network was trained, to begin with, by imitation, on millions of moves from games between strong human players. That made it play like a competent human. Then it was improved by reinforcement learning, playing against copies of itself, millions of times, and being rewarded for winning. It was this stage of self-play that carried it beyond its human teachers, and that produced move thirty-seven.

A year later, DeepMind dispensed with the human games altogether. The successor, AlphaGo Zero, began with nothing but the rules, and random play. It learnt entirely by playing against itself. Within three days it was able to beat the version that had beaten Lee Sedol by a hundred games to nil. Along the way it rediscovered much of the opening theory that human players had built up over centuries, and then gave some of it up in favour of ideas of its own.

That result made a deep impression on the field, and it is easy to see why. Here was proof that a system could go beyond all human knowledge of a domain, given two things: a clear signal of success, and the ability to practise without limit. It was natural to ask whether the same could be done for language and for reasoning. But games are a special case. In Go, the rules are fixed; a game can be simulated perfectly at great speed; and at the end there is no doubt about who has won. Most of what we would like an intelligent system to do has none of those properties.

Yann LeCun proposed a metaphor in 2016 which has turned out to describe pretty well what the laboratories ended up doing. If intelligence is a cake, he said, then self-supervised learning is the body of the cake, supervised learning is the icing, and reinforcement learning is the cherry on the top. Pre-training on next-token prediction supplies the bulk: a broad knowledge of the world, obtained cheaply from a vast quantity of data. Reinforcement learning is costly and inefficient, and you would not want to learn everything by means of it. But applied on top of a pre-trained model, which already knows a great deal, it can do something that imitation cannot do. It can teach the model to seek a goal.

Thinking out loud

Before we can see how the two were put together, we need one more idea, which came from a simple observation about how language models go wrong.

Ask a language model of around 2022 a question that needs several steps of reasoning, a word problem in arithmetic, say, and require it to give the answer straight away, and it would often be wrong. But if you added to the prompt a phrase such as "let's think step by step", so that the model wrote out its working before it gave its answer, its accuracy went up dramatically. This is called chain-of-thought prompting, and when it was first discovered it seemed almost like a magic spell.

There is a good architectural reason for it, and you now have the means to understand it. Consider what a Transformer does in order to produce one token. Information goes up through the stack of layers, once, and a token comes out. If the model has a hundred layers, then there are a hundred steps of computation and no more. There are no loops. The model cannot decide to think for longer about a hard question. Whatever it is going to work out before it commits itself to the next token must be worked out in one pass of a fixed depth. Some problems simply need more sequential steps than there are layers.

But the model generates its text one token at a time, and each token that it produces becomes part of its own input. So if it writes down an intermediate result, then when it comes to the next token, it can attend to that result, and build on it. The text that it has written serves as a scratchpad, an external working memory. Each token is another pass through the network, and so a chain of reasoning written out over a thousand tokens gives the model a thousand passes in which to do its computing, in place of one. The loop which the architecture lacks is supplied by the process of writing.

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman drew a famous distinction between two modes of human thinking: a fast, automatic, intuitive one, and a slow, deliberate, effortful one. A single forward pass through the network is the fast kind. It is a matter of intuition, of pattern recognition; it is what you do when you see that two plus two is four. A chain of thought is the slow kind. It is what you do when you multiply two three-digit numbers with pencil and paper.

Reasoning models

At first, chain of thought was a trick that users applied. The next step was to build it into the model, and to train the model to be good at it. This is where reinforcement learning comes back in.

The recipe is roughly as follows. Take a pre-trained language model. Give it a large supply of problems whose answers can be checked automatically: mathematical problems with a known solution; programming tasks, where you can run the code and see whether the tests pass. Let the model think, at whatever length it chooses, writing out its reasoning before it gives a final answer. Then check the answer. If it is right, reinforce the whole chain of reasoning that led up to it: adjust the weights so that thinking in that manner becomes more probable. If it is wrong, discourage it.

Look at what is and is not being specified. Nobody tells the model how it should reason. There are no examples of good reasoning for it to copy. The single signal is whether it arrived at the right answer. It is the same arrangement as in AlphaGo: a policy, a clear criterion of success, and a great deal of practice. And what the model finds out, through that practice, are the habits of thought that actually lead to correct answers. It learns to break a problem down into parts. It learns to try an approach, to notice that it is not working, and to go back. It learns to check its own work. It learns these things not because they were demonstrated to it, but because they succeed.

The first model of this type to be widely available was released by OpenAI in September 2024, under the name o1. Within a few months, every major laboratory had its counterpart. These are known as reasoning models, and the difference in use is plain: put a hard question to one of them, and it may think for some seconds, or for many minutes, generating thousands of tokens of private deliberation before it says anything. On hard problems in mathematics, science and programming, the improvement was dramatic. By the summer of 2025, experimental models from two of the laboratories had attained the standard of a gold medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad, a competition for the most gifted school-age mathematicians in the world.

Two consequences of this are worth pointing out.

The first is that it opened up a new dimension of scaling. Up to that point, the way to get a better answer had been to train a larger model. Now there was a second way: let the same model think for longer. This is referred to as test-time compute, or inference-time compute, and it changes the economics. Intelligence becomes, to a degree, a dial that you can turn up at the moment of use, by paying for more thought.

The second is that it gives, at least in principle, an answer to the question that I raised at the start of the chapter. A model trained in this way is no longer just imitating human text. It is being optimised against reality: against whether the proof is valid, or the program runs. And so, in domains in which success can be checked, there is no evident reason that it should stop at the human level, just as AlphaGo did not stop there.

The catch lies in that qualification: domains in which success can be checked. Mathematics and programming are, like Go, unusually clear-cut. Much of what matters is not. Was that a good research strategy? Was that the right diagnosis? Was it a sensible business decision? The feedback is slow, noisy and disputed. To construct training environments with dependable reward signals for fuzzier and longer tasks is now one of the main occupations of the laboratories, and a small industry has grown up to supply them.

Agents

Put together the elements that we now have. A model that can reason through a problem in a number of steps. The ability, from the last chapter, to use tools: to run code, search the web, and read and write files. And a loop in which the model takes an action, observes what comes of it, and decides on its next step. The result is an agent: a system which, given a goal, works towards it more or less on its own, over many steps.

The first field in which agents have become truly useful is software engineering, and that is no accident. Code is text, which is the model's native medium. There is a vast amount of it to train on. And, crucially, it can be checked: you can run a program and see whether it works, which provides a clear signal both for training the model and for the agent to correct itself as it goes. In the space of about two years, these systems have gone from completing a line of code to carrying out, largely unsupervised, tasks that would take a competent programmer hours. A considerable share of the new code at the AI laboratories themselves is now written by their own models.

Why did this take longer to arrive than chatbots? The reason is arithmetic, and it is important. Suppose that a model takes the right step ninety-nine times out of a hundred. That sounds excellent. But a task of a hundred steps, in which every step must be right, will then succeed only about a third of the time. Over long tasks, small rates of error are compounded mercilessly. An agent has to be extremely reliable at each step, or else good at noticing its own mistakes and recovering from them, and for a long time the models were neither.

This suggests a way of measuring progress that is more informative than most. A research organisation called METR has proposed that, in place of asking how hard a question a model can answer, we should ask how long a task it can complete: long, that is, in terms of the time that the task would take a skilled human being. They assembled a collection of tasks, mostly in software, ranging from a few seconds of human effort to many hours, and measured the length of task at which each generation of model succeeded half of the time. For the models of 2019, it was a few seconds. For those of 2023, several minutes. By 2025, it was a matter of hours. Plotted against the date of release, on a logarithmic scale, the points lie on a straight line, once again. The length of task that these systems can manage has been doubling about every seven months, and in the most recent period, if anything, faster.

You should treat this, like all extrapolations, with caution. The tasks are mostly well-defined software problems, and not the untidy, ill-specified work that fills most people's days; and success half of the time is a long way from dependability. But if the trend should continue, it implies systems that can carry out, unaided, tasks that take a human being weeks, within a few years. A large part of the argument about the near future of this technology is an argument about whether that line will go on.

What is still missing

I do not wish to leave you with the impression that these systems are simply human-like minds that have not quite grown up. They are oddly shaped. In some respects they are superhuman: in the breadth of their knowledge, in speed, in the ability to keep a very large body of text in view at once. In other respects they are deficient in ways that would be astonishing in a person of comparable abilities.

The deficiency most often remarked upon is in memory and learning. A human being who starts in a new job is not much use on the first day, and is far more use after six months, having picked up, on the job, a great mass of specific knowledge: how this organisation works, what was tried before and did not succeed, what this colleague means when she says a thing in a certain way. A language model does not do this. Its weights are fixed. Everything that it learns in the course of a task is held in its context window, and when the task is over, that is discarded. The next session starts from nothing. There are workarounds: the model may write notes for itself, and read them the next time. But nobody has yet found a way for these systems to learn continually from experience in the sturdy and efficient way that people do. This goes by the name of the continual learning problem, and a number of prominent researchers hold that it is the most important thing standing between the systems of today and ones that could truly take the place of a human worker.

There are others. The models still find it hard to keep hold of the thread over very long tasks. They can be brittle when they meet with something truly unlike their training. Their judgment, their sense of what is worth doing, is behind their skill at doing it. And we have only an imperfect understanding of why they do what they do.

That last point brings us to the subject of the next chapter. We have spent eleven chapters on how these systems are built and trained. We have said very little about what actually goes on inside one of them when it runs. There are those hundred billion weights; what are they doing? For most of the history of the field, the honest answer was that nobody knew. That has begun, slowly, to change.

Key terms

  • Policy: a function from situation to a probability distribution over actions. In an LLM, the model itself is the policy; its "actions" are tokens.
  • Reward: the scalar feedback signal. Value function: predicted future reward from a state.
  • Self-play: generating training data by having the agent play against itself.
  • Reward hacking / specification gaming: maximising the stated reward in ways the designer did not intend.
  • Chain of thought (CoT): writing out intermediate reasoning before answering.
  • Reasoning model: a model trained with RL to use extended chains of thought.
  • Test-time / inference-time compute: computation spent thinking at the moment of use, as opposed to during training.
  • Agent: model + tools + a loop.
  • Time horizon (METR): the human-time length of tasks a model completes with 50% success.
  • Continual learning: learning durably from experience after deployment; currently unsolved.

The maths in brief

  • Policy gradient (REINFORCE): ∇J = E[ R · Σₜ ∇ log π(aₜ | sₜ) ]. Raise the log-probability of the actions taken, in proportion to the reward R that followed.
  • Compounding error: with per-step success p, an n-step task succeeds with probability pⁿ. 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.37; 0.999¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.90.
